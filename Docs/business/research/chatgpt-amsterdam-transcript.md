**You:** Day 1, I'm walking along a canal and there's a really narrow house, like one window wide, with a hook sticking out at the top. What is this and what's the story?

**ChatGPT:**
What is that hook at the top?

It's called a hijsbalk — a hoisting beam, usually fitted with a hook and pulley. It's one of Amsterdam's most distinctive architectural features.

Here's the story: The houses were built tall and narrow. Land along the canals was valuable, and building plots were often narrow, so homes grew upward and backward rather than sideways. The staircases were incredibly steep. Getting a wardrobe, mattress, or piano up them was often impossible. The hook solved the problem. Residents attached a rope and pulley, hoisted furniture up the outside of the building, and brought it in through an upper window. Look at the angle of the façade. Many houses lean slightly forward at the top so suspended furniture can clear the wall and windows. The clever part is that these hooks aren't just historical decoration. The same principle is still used to move large furniture into Amsterdam's old canal houses today.

And is it the narrowest house? Possibly! Two famous buildings are easy to confuse:
- Singel 7 — the famously tiny façade. This building appears to be barely wider than a doorway. The twist is that the extremely narrow part is actually the back of a larger house.
- Oude Hoogstraat 22 — the genuinely tiny house. Only about two metres wide and roughly five metres deep.
