# 08 — Paulo, listening in his second language

**Who.** 31, from São Paulo. Works in English every day and is comfortable in it. Comfortable is
not fluent: a fast narrator, a French street name inside an English sentence, three kings in one
clause, or an idiom he has never met will each cost him the sentence they are in and usually the
one after. He is not embarrassed about this and he is not going to switch to a Portuguese tour,
because there isn't one worth having.

**The request.** Start Place de la Bastille, end Hôtel de Ville, two and a half hours, interest:
history. Friday, 10:00, cold and dry.

**What he needs and never types in.** Time to hear a thing twice. A transcript. Words he can look
up without losing his place. And a narrator who is not being charming at speed.

## The two and a half hours

1. 10:00–10:04 — Under the July Column at the Bastille. Before walking anywhere he opens the
   settings and drops the playback speed to 0.9. **The first thing he does to the tour is make it
   longer.** Every piece in the day is now 11% above its stated length before he replays a word of
   it.
2. 10:04–10:16 — Walks up the Rue Saint-Antoine, listening. He loses the thread twice — once on
   "faubourg" and once on a run of three royal names in a single sentence — and rewinds neither,
   because rewinding on a narrow pavement means stopping and being walked into. **That content is
   simply gone.** Camille gets these minutes free; for Paulo they are the most expensive listening
   of the day.
3. 10:16–10:34 — Place des Vosges. Eighteen minutes. Six of them are the narration and four more
   are him playing the middle third again, standing still, now that he can hold the phone and look
   at the screen. **The re-listen is not a repair. It is how he consumes the tour**, and it needs
   to be in the plan rather than eating it.
4. 10:34–10:39 — Walks through to the Hôtel de Sully.
5. 10:39–10:52 — Thirteen minutes, and he never presses play. The piece is dense with dates, and
   he reads English faster than he hears it, so he reads the transcript instead. **All the content
   is delivered and the audio ran for none of it.**
6. 10:52–11:00 — Walks toward Saint-Paul.
7. 11:00–11:20 — Twenty minutes in the Village Saint-Paul courtyards, only five of which have any
   audio. He buys a pastry and sends a photograph home. **Content-light minutes rest him in a way
   they do not rest a native listener** — he has been doing translation for an hour and he can
   feel it.
8. 11:20–11:26 — Walks to the church.
9. 11:26–11:52 — Saint-Paul-Saint-Louis, twenty-six minutes, twelve of them inside. The narration
   says "the Jesuit facade". He does not know the English word, though he knows it perfectly well
   in Portuguese, and he stops to look it up. Ninety seconds, and when he restarts he is a
   paragraph behind and has to scrub back. **One unglossed word costs far more than the sentence
   holding it.**
10. 11:52–12:02 — Walks west along the Rue de Rivoli.
11. 12:02–12:18 — Sixteen minutes in front of the Hôtel de Ville. He plays the whole seven-minute
    piece, then plays it again from the top. Second time through he gets nearly all of it, and
    this is the stop he will describe to people at home. **Seven minutes of audio occupied
    fourteen minutes of stop, and that was the tour working, not failing.**
12. 12:18–12:30 — Sits with a coffee. Twelve minutes, audio off. He is tired in a way no other
    persona here is tired: **listening in a second language is work, and it accumulates.**

## The arithmetic

**Walking: 41 minutes. At places: 109 minutes.** Audio published across the day: about 31 minutes.
Audio time actually consumed, counting the 0.9× speed and two full replays: about 46. **The stated
length of the narration understates his listening by roughly half.**

## What this persona breaks

- **Narration length is not narration cost.** Every model in the system prices a piece by its
  duration in seconds. For Paulo a seven-minute piece is a fourteen-minute event, and the
  multiplier depends on the writing, not the length.
- **A re-listen has to be planned time.** A stop lasts the longer of its visit time and its
  narration. His stops need to last about one and a half times the narration, and the
  half is not silence — it is the same words again.
- **The walk/talk overlap is worth less to him than to anyone else.** The engine already treats
  narration heard while walking as free minutes, and for Camille it is. Paulo cannot rewind on the
  move, so a leg is the one place where missed content stays missed. **The cheapest minutes in the
  model are his most expensive.**
- **A transcript is a delivery channel, not an accessibility footnote** (step 5). One of his stops
  delivered its content entirely in text. Nothing today plans for a stop where the audio never
  plays and the visitor is not skipping it.
- **Density is a written property the planner never sees.** Words per minute, unexplained proper
  nouns, three names in one clause, jokes, idiom. **Two four-minute pieces are not the same
  product**, and the difference between them decides whether he understands the stop.
- **Fatigue comes from the audio, not the legs.** Rosemary tires because she walked; Nadia's
  afternoon ends because a five-year-old ran out. Paulo has walked 41 easy minutes and is worn out
  by the tour itself. A plan that back-loads its densest content is worse than one that front-loads
  it, and nothing expresses that.
