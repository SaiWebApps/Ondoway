# Fact sheet — Castle Clinton & the Statue of Liberty
**For:** 8-minute audio deep dive, heard in the ferry queue at Castle Clinton, Battery Park.
**Compiled:** 2026-09-22. **Source rule:** NPS / LOC / NARA / Statue of Liberty–Ellis Island Foundation / WPA Guide only. No commercial guidebooks.

**How to read this:** each line is one checkable fact — claim, then source, URL, and source date.
Lines marked **[CONFLICT]** are numbers where government sources disagree; the disagreement is described, not hidden.
Lines marked **[UNVERIFIED]** could not be confirmed to the source standard — do not narrate these as fact.

A note on the 1954 handbook: *Statue of Liberty National Monument*, NPS Historical Handbook Series No. 11, by Benjamin Levine and Isabelle F. Story (Washington: National Park Service, 1954). It is a US Government work and public domain. NPS retired its own `parkhistory/online_books` copy during this research; the intact mirror used here is npshistory.com. ## 1. Where they are standing — Castle Clinton

### The fort
1. The fort was built on rocks off the southwest tip of Manhattan, originally as the **Southwest Battery** — the name "West Battery" is also used in period sources. — NPS, "History & Culture, Castle Clinton" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/index.htm (accessed 2026-09-22)
2. Construction ran **1808–1811** — just in time for the War of 1812. — NPS, "History & Culture, Castle Clinton" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/index.htm
3. **[CONFLICT — construction start]** NPS gives both **1807–1811** (park fact sheet, and the page titled "Southwest Battery/Castle Clinton 1807-1823") and **1808–1811** (park history page, and the Southwest Battery place page). Safest narration: **"finished in 1811"** — which every NPS page agrees on. The fort was **completed before 2 June 1812**, when it was garrisoned. — NPS, "Fact Sheet" https://www.nps.gov/npnh/learn/news/fact-sheet-cacl.htm ; NPS, "Southwest Battery/Castle Clinton 1807-1823" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/southwest-battery-castle-clinton-1807-1823.htm
4. It was designed by **Lt. Col. Jonathan Williams**, the first Commandant of West Point. — NPS, "Fact Sheet: Castle Clinton National Monument" https://www.nps.gov/npnh/learn/news/fact-sheet-cacl.htm
5. It was armed with **28 cannons**. — NPS, "History & Culture, Castle Clinton" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/index.htm
6. Each cannon could throw a **32-pound cannonball about 1.5 miles**. — NPS, "History & Culture, Castle Clinton" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/index.htm
7. On **27 March 1812**, General Joseph Bloomfield was appointed to command all the fortifications in New York City. — NPS, "History & Culture, Castle Clinton" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/index.htm
8. **It never fired a shot in anger — and NPS says so outright.** The fort **"never witnessed any action against the enemy, only ever firing to celebrate anniversaries and for target practice."** — NPS, "Southwest Battery/Castle Clinton 1807-1823" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/southwest-battery-castle-clinton-1807-1823.htm
8a. The reason is the good part: **"none of New York's harbor defense forts, including the Southwest Battery, fired a shot throughout the War of 1812."** The defences worked by existing. — NPS, "Southwest Battery (Castle Clinton)" https://www.nps.gov/places/southwest-battery.htm
8b. **The architect alongside Williams was John McComb, Jr.** — who also co-designed New York's City Hall. — NPS, "Southwest Battery/Castle Clinton 1807-1823" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/southwest-battery-castle-clinton-1807-1823.htm
8c. **It was an island fort reached by a 200-foot wooden causeway and a drawbridge** — the listener is standing on ground that did not exist. The water between here and the fort was filled in later. — NPS, "Southwest Battery/Castle Clinton 1807-1823" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/southwest-battery-castle-clinton-1807-1823.htm
8d. The entrance was an **11-foot-tall sally port** opening onto the parade ground. — NPS, "Southwest Battery/Castle Clinton 1807-1823" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/southwest-battery-castle-clinton-1807-1823.htm
8e. **28 embrasures** — gun ports — for 28 cannons firing 32-pound balls up to 1.5 miles, served by a **hotshot furnace** for heating cannonballs red-hot to set enemy ships alight. — NPS, "Southwest Battery/Castle Clinton 1807-1823" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/southwest-battery-castle-clinton-1807-1823.htm
8f. **It was garrisoned from 2 June 1812**, with Captain Campbell's Company and later New York State Artillery; optimal operation needed **at least 120 artillerymen and 40 infantrymen.** — NPS, "Southwest Battery/Castle Clinton 1807-1823" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/southwest-battery-castle-clinton-1807-1823.htm
8g. **The harbour ring, and this is the line that ties the whole chapter together:** the Southwest Battery worked with **Castle Williams on Governors Island, Fort Wood on Bedloe's Island, and Fort Gibson on Oyster Island.** **Fort Wood is the star-shaped fort the Statue of Liberty now stands inside. Oyster Island is Ellis Island.** All three points of the family's day were originally gun positions in one defensive ring. — NPS, "Southwest Battery/Castle Clinton 1807-1823" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/southwest-battery-castle-clinton-1807-1823.htm
9. The **Army vacated the fort in 1821**. — NPS, "Fact Sheet: Castle Clinton National Monument" https://www.nps.gov/npnh/learn/news/fact-sheet-cacl.htm

9a. **The origin document:** the harbour survey was "begun late in 1805 by **Lt. Col. Jonathan Williams** of the United States Engineers"; his commission's *Outline of a Plan of Defense*, submitted **July 1807**, called for "two casemated batteries in the water, one about 50 feet from the west head of the Battery." — Thomas M. Pitkin, *Historic Structures Report, Castle Clinton, Part I*, NPS, 6 May 1960, pp. 8–9
9b. **The land under the water was formally ceded:** "Map of Land under water ceded by the Corporation of New York to the United States for Fortifications. 1807," Map no. 49, Fortifications File, Office of the Chief of Engineers — **National Archives**. — Pitkin HSR, plate 1
9c. **[IMPORTANT — NPS is more cautious about the designer than the popular account.]** "Although Colonel Jonathan Williams was the Army Engineer in charge of all fortification construction in New York Harbor during the years immediately preceding the War of 1812, **the evidence is not conclusive that he actually designed and superintended the construction of Castle Clinton.**" John McComb, Jr. was "associated with the construction work, at least in the role of contractor," and the monumental sallyport gateway **"was his work."** Say "built under Jonathan Williams" rather than "designed by." — Pitkin HSR, NPS, 1960, pp. 14–15
9d. **Williams quit over the budget.** Criticised by Washington in **June 1810** "because the cost of constructing the fort [was] considerably more than his original estimate, Williams asked to be relieved from his position as Engineer-in-Charge in New York harbor." — Pitkin HSR, p. 15
9e. **The fort's first shots were a salute, not a battle:** "The West Battery was not completed until the fall of 1811; a salute [was] fired from the new fort on **November 25, Evacuation Day.**" — Pitkin HSR, p. 10
9f. **The architecture, precisely:** "a one-tiered, open casemated fort of **reddish brownstone**, mounting 28 guns… **circular in design, with an inner radius of 92 feet**… Exterior walls are **8 feet thick**." — NPS, National Register nomination (Millman & Weible, Feb 1983/Mar 1984)
9g. **Why the walls are so absurdly thick:** they "were designed to carry **additional tiers of casemates, which were never built.**" The fort is overbuilt for a second and third storey that never came. — Holland & Jones, *Special History Study: Masonry Forts of the National Park Service*, NPS, August 1973, p. 162
9h. **Fourteen casemates, two guns each:** "Each casemate [was] armed with two 32-pound battering cannon, cast iron, mounted on wooden carriages" — **14 × 2 = the 28 guns.** — Pitkin HSR, pp. 77, 100
9i. **What the garrison actually did all summer in 1812:** "exercising the gunners and in practice firing at a target, **an old hulk anchored in the harbor off the Battery.**" — Pitkin HSR, pp. 10–11
9j. **The honest NPS answer to "why did it never fire?"** — "The city had been saved from bombardment and invasion, **whether because [the strength] of its defenses had deterred an enemy attack, or because the British were content merely to cripple the port by a blockade.**" NPS declines to claim the deterrent worked. That intellectual honesty is worth repeating on air. — Pitkin HSR, p. 12
9k. **Castle Williams is its literal twin, and the pairing is the point.** Castle Clinton = **West Battery**; Castle Williams on Governors Island = **East Battery**. Built in the same years (1807–1811), the pair covered the Hudson channel between them as part of the **"Second American System."** — NPS, "Castle Williams" https://www.nps.gov/gois/learn/historyculture/castle-williams.htm
9l. **The causeway, measured:** the fort sat "on a manmade island of stone about 200 feet off the 'west head' of the Battery, to which it was joined by a **wooden causeway and drawbridge**"; the causeway was **"from 222 to 224 feet long and 26 to 30 feet wide,"** with the drawbridge section about 20 feet from the counterguard. — NPS National Register nomination; Pitkin HSR, p. 25
9m. **The end of military use:** Totten's survey concluded the fort "may be struck out of the present system of defence without materially weakening it"; the War Department ordered it turned over **18 February 1823**, stores removed by 19 April, and **"on June 23, 1823, the fort was formally reconveyed to a committee of the Common Council."** — Pitkin HSR, pp. 12–13

### When the water disappeared — the ground the family is standing on
9n. **It was still an island in 1848:** "By 1848 a proposal had been made to incorporate Castle Garden, **then still surrounded by water**, into Manhattan and thus to increase the Battery area to about twenty-four acres." — Svejda, NPS, 1968, p. 54
9o. **The fill was under way by 1854:** the lease stipulated that "the contractors for enlarging the Battery could not close up the passageway on the bridge, but were only allowed to fill up around it, on condition that they not obstruct the passage." — Svejda, NPS, 1968, p. 51
9p. **The dates:** "Around this time, the park was extended further by landfills to an area of about ten acres. **Another massive landfill project got underway in 1853 and was completed in 1872.**" — NYC Parks, "The Battery" https://www.nycgovparks.org/parks/battery-park/history
9q. **Bottom line, and it is a genuinely good "look down" beat:** Castle Garden **stopped being an island in the mid-1850s** — fill begun 1853, filled around the causeway by 1854, complete in time for the immigration depot in 1855. The wider Battery Park landfill ran to **1872**. **The family is standing on water.** Everyone who arrived here before about 1855 crossed a drawbridge to get in.

### The name
10. In **1817** the fort was renamed **Castle Clinton**, honoring **DeWitt Clinton**, the city's wartime mayor. — NPS, "History & Culture, Castle Clinton" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/index.htm
11. **[CONFLICT — RESOLVED: use 1815.]** Two NPS pages say 1817, two say 1815. **1815 wins, on internal evidence.** The NPS Historic Structures Report narrates General Alexander Macomb establishing his headquarters at "Castle Clinton," theretofore designated West Battery: **"This is the first known [use] of the name, probably chosen to honor DeWitt Clinton, who had just retired as Mayor of New York."** — Thomas M. Pitkin, *Historic Structures Report, Castle Clinton, Part I*, NPS, 6 May 1960, pp. 12–13
11a. **The clincher:** **DeWitt Clinton left the mayoralty in 1815.** By 1817 he was Governor — so "just retired as Mayor" only works for 1815. The HSR also catalogues a plan already titled "A Plan of Castle Clinton, New York," c. 1817, i.e. the name already existed by then. — Pitkin HSR, NPS, 1960
11b. **DeWitt Clinton was mayor during the war and later Governor** — and the man behind the Erie Canal, which is why New York, not Boston or Philadelphia, became the port every immigrant aimed at. — NPS, "Southwest Battery (Castle Clinton)" https://www.nps.gov/places/southwest-battery.htm (mayor/governor per NPS; the Erie Canal link is context, not an NPS claim)

### Castle Garden — the pleasure ground
12. **By June 1823 it was "fully demilitarized and officially transferred from the federal government to the city of New York."** — NPS, "Southwest Battery/Castle Clinton 1807-1823" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/southwest-battery-castle-clinton-1807-1823.htm
12a. In **1823** the fort was **deeded to New York City**. — NPS, "History & Culture, Castle Clinton" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/index.htm
13. The following summer (**1824**) it reopened as a restaurant and entertainment centre, renamed **Castle Garden**. — NPS, "History & Culture, Castle Clinton" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/index.htm
14. **A roof was added in the 1840s** — turning an open-air garden into a hall. — NPS, "History & Culture, Castle Clinton" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/index.htm
15. It was **redesigned as an opera house in 1844**. — NPS, "Fact Sheet: Castle Clinton National Monument" https://www.nps.gov/npnh/learn/news/fact-sheet-cacl.htm
16. **Jenny Lind made her American debut here in 1850.** — NPS, "History & Culture, Castle Clinton" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/index.htm
16a. **The exact date: 11 September 1850.** "On September 11, 1850 Jenny Lind made her American debut at Castle Garden… **Over five thousand attended her first concert.**" — NPS, "Castle Garden Entertainment Center 1823–1854" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/castle-garden-entertainment-center.htm
16b. **P. T. Barnum's own account agrees on the crowd:** "although **about five thousand persons** were present at the first concert, their entrance was marked with as much order and quiet as was ever witnessed in the assembling of a congregation at church." — *Struggles and Triumphs; or, Forty Years' Recollections of P. T. Barnum*, 1869, p. 298. **Public domain.**
16c. **The ticket auction — the thing nobody knows.** "Jenny Lind's first concert was fixed to come off at Castle Garden, on Wednesday evening, September 11th, and **most of the tickets were sold at auction** on the Saturday and Monday previous to the concert." — Barnum, 1869, p. 294
16d. **The first ticket, and the man who bought it:** "**John N. Genin, the hatter, laid the foundation of his fortune by purchasing the first ticket at $225.**" — Barnum, 1869, p. 294
16e. **The scale of the auction:** "**three thousand people** were present at the auction. One thousand tickets were sold on the first day for an aggregate sum of **$10,141**." — Barnum, 1869, pp. 294–295
16f. **Quotable, Barnum on what Genin bought — this is a superb short story inside the story:** "'Who is the bidder?' said the auctioneer, as he knocked down that ticket at Castle Garden. '**Genin, the hatter**,' was the response… 'Who is Genin, the hatter?' they exclaimed. They had never heard of him before… **Men throughout the country involuntarily took off their hats to see if they had a 'Genin' hat on their heads.**" — Barnum, 1869, pp. 494–495. A man bought one concert ticket and made himself a household name.
16g. **Genin was not even the record.** First-ticket prices by city: "**John N. Genin, in New York, $225; Ossian E. Dodge, in Boston, $625; Col. William C. Ross, in Providence, $650; M. A. Root, in Philadelphia, $625.**" — Barnum, 1869, p. 368
16h. **Ordinary prices, for contrast:** "The fixed price of tickets ranged from **$7 to $3**. Promenade tickets were from $2 to $1 each." — Barnum, 1869, p. 368
16i. **What she sang:** "before she had sung a dozen notes of **'Casta Diva,'** she began to recover her self-possession… the remainder of the air was drowned in a perfect tempest of acclamation." — Barnum, 1869, p. 298
16j. **The commissioned anthem:** "Before her arrival I had offered **$200 for a prize ode, 'Greeting to America,'** to be sung by Jenny Lind at her first concert. Several hundred 'poems' were sent in… The prize was awarded to **Bayard Taylor**." Music by **Julius Benedict**. — Barnum, 1869, pp. 290–291
16k. **The curtain calls:** "At the conclusion of the concert Jenny Lind was loudly called for, and was obliged to appear **three times** before the audience could be satisfied. **They then called vociferously for 'Barnum.'**" — Barnum, 1869, pp. 298–299
16l. **What Barnum paid her — from the contract reprinted in his own book:** **$1,000 per concert for 150 concerts**, Barnum paying "every expense appertaining to the concerts," plus first-class travel, a carriage, two servants and a companion; conductor **Julius Benedict £5,000**, baritone **Giovanni Belletti £2,500** — the whole sum secured **in advance with Messrs. Baring Brothers, of London.** — Barnum, 1869, pp. 276–279
16m. **The number to say aloud: $187,500, deposited before she sang a note.** "it would be requisite for me to place the entire amount stipulated, **$187,500**, in the hands of the London bankers." — Barnum, 1869, p. 281
16n. **Barnum then voluntarily improved her deal** after the first concert: "you shall receive not only $1,000 for each concert, besides all the expenses, as heretofore agreed on, but after taking $5,500 per night for expenses and my services, **the balance shall be equally divided between us.**" — Barnum, 1869, p. 295
16o. **Receipts:** first concert **$17,864.05**; second, 13 September, **$14,203.03**. The "first regular concert" was Tuesday, 17 September 1850. Whole tour: **"Total Receipts of 95 Concerts $712,161.34."** — Barnum, 1869, pp. 299, 368
16p. **And she gave her share away.** "On Tuesday, September 10th, I informed Miss Lind that… her portion of the proceeds of the first concert would amount to $10,000. **She immediately resolved to devote every dollar of it to charity.**" — Barnum, 1869, p. 297
16q. **[CONFLICT — her charity figure]** Barnum says **$10,000**; NYC Parks says **$12,600**. Barnum is primary but wrote 19 years later and his arithmetic shifts. **Safest line: "she gave every dollar of her share away."**
16r. **The other guests of this room:** Lafayette welcomed 1824, **Louis Kossuth 1851**, **Edward VII, then Prince of Wales, 1860**; also Andrew Jackson (June 1833) and Presidents Van Buren, Tyler, Polk and Fillmore; Samuel Colt's demonstrations; Charles Durant's balloon ascensions. — NPS, "Castle Garden Entertainment Center"; Svejda, NPS, 1968, p. 53
16s. **Lafayette, with a crowd figure:** "General the Marquis de LaFayette, upon landing in New York in 1824, was welcomed officially at Castle Garden by **about 6,000 New Yorkers**." First appearance **16 August 1824**; "On September 14th, a grand reception was held in his honor." — Svejda, NPS, 1968, p. 53; NPS, "Castle Garden Entertainment Center"
16t. **[CONFLICT — the telegraph, and the WPA Guide is wrong.]** The WPA Guide says Morse demonstrated the telegraph here in **1835**. NPS sources say **1842**: "In 1842 Morse demonstrated his telegraph there" — a cable laid from Castle Garden to Governors Island. **Use 1842.** The WPA's 1835 conflates Morse's NYU prototype with the harbour demonstration; the 1946 Senate Report's "first demonstration of the telegraph" is likewise an overstatement. — Svejda, NPS, 1968, p. 53; NPS National Register nomination
16u. **The opera house conversion:** "Major alterations were undertaken in **1844–45** with the conversion of the building to a theater: additions included a roof, flooring, galleries, permanent seating, and a stage." On reopening in 1845 "the Italian opera performed **Rossini's *Semiramide***." — NPS National Register nomination; NPS, "Castle Garden Entertainment Center"
16v. **LPC's version:** "In 1845 it was covered by a domed roof and served as the City's principal hall for the performance of opera and concerts." — LPC, *Castle Clinton* LP-0029, 23 November 1965
17. It operated as a theatre **until 1854**. — NPS, "History & Culture, Castle Clinton" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/index.htm

### The immigrant landing depot
18. **Castle Garden opened as an immigrant landing depot on 3 August 1855.** — NPS, "History & Culture, Castle Clinton" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/index.htm
18a. **[NOT a conflict — two different events.]** "it was only on August 1, 1855, that Castle Garden was formally opened and assumed its role as a landing depot," and **"The first immigrants to land at Castle Garden arrived on Friday, August 3, 1855."** Say "opened 1 August; the first immigrants walked in on 3 August." — Svejda, NPS, 1968, pp. 54, 66
19. Over the next **34 years, more than 8 million people** entered the United States through Castle Garden. — NPS, "History & Culture, Castle Clinton" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/index.htm
20. **Two out of every three** immigrants to the United States in this period passed through Castle Garden. — NPS, "History & Culture, Castle Clinton" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/index.htm
21. More precisely: between **August 1855 and December 1889, roughly 8.28 million of the 10.96 million** immigrants entering the US — about **75%** — were processed at Castle Garden. — NPS, "Castle Garden Emigrant Depot" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/castle-garden-emigrant-depot.htm
22. **[CONFLICT — the share]** "Two out of three" (≈67%) and "8.28 of 10.96 million" (≈75%) are both NPS, on two pages. Either say "about two in three" or "about three in four" — but not both, and do not average them. **Recommendation: use three in four**, because the precise figures (**8,280,917 of 10,956,910**) are the ones NPS states explicitly and repeats. — NPS, "Castle Garden Emigrant Depot" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/castle-garden-emigrant-depot.htm
23. NPS calls it **"America's first facility dedicated entirely to the welfare of immigrants coming to America."** — NPS, "Castle Garden Emigrant Depot" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/castle-garden-emigrant-depot.htm
24. It was built to protect arrivals from exploitation — runners, sharpers and fraudulent ticket sellers who preyed on people landing at open wharves. — NPS, "Castle Garden Emigrant Depot" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/castle-garden-emigrant-depot.htm
25. Services inside: **railroad ticket sales, currency exchange, baggage storage, family reunification, letter writing, medical care, employment placement, bathing facilities, food, and referrals to approved boardinghouses.** — NPS, "Castle Garden Emigrant Depot" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/castle-garden-emigrant-depot.htm
26. The largest groups, in order of frequency: **Germans, then Irish, English, Swedish, Italians, Scottish, Russians, Norwegians, Swiss and French.** — NPS, "Castle Garden Emigrant Depot" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/castle-garden-emigrant-depot.htm
27. **About 20% of Americans today can trace an ancestor through this building.** This is the line that lands with a family in the queue. — NPS, "Castle Garden Emigrant Depot" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/castle-garden-emigrant-depot.htm
28. The depot kept detailed records of names, vessels, destinations, finances and family contacts — **but most of the original records were destroyed.** — NPS, "Castle Garden Emigrant Depot" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/castle-garden-emigrant-depot.htm
29. It **closed on 18 April 1890**. — NPS, "History & Culture, Castle Clinton" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/index.htm
30. **[CONFLICT — RESOLVED: use 18 April 1890.]** NPS web pages give both 30 April and 18 April. The dedicated NPS monograph settles it: Treasury Secretary Windom revoked the contract effective "sixty days hence, on April 18, 1890," and **"On Friday, April 18, 1890, Castle Garden received immigrants for the last time, and two steamers, the *Bohemia* and the *State of Indiana*, bringing in 465 immigrants, were the last to land."** Federal control began at the Barge Office on Saturday 19 April 1890 under Superintendent John B. Weber. **18 April 1890 was in fact a Friday** — the day-of-week check confirms it. — George J. Svejda, *Castle Garden as an Immigrant Depot, 1855–1890*, National Park Service, 2 December 1968, pp. 133, 143 (citing *New York Times*, 19 April 1890)
30a. **The last two ships have names, which is gold for narration:** the ***Bohemia*** and the ***State of Indiana***, **465 people**, the afternoon of Friday 18 April 1890. The door closed on this building with a countable number of people walking through it. — Svejda, NPS, 1968, p. 143 — NPS, "Castle Garden Emigrant Depot" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/castle-garden-emigrant-depot.htm
31. Immigration operations moved to **Ellis Island over 1890–1892**. — NPS, "Fact Sheet: Castle Clinton National Monument" https://www.nps.gov/npnh/learn/news/fact-sheet-cacl.htm

### Aquarium, demolition, rescue
32. The building reopened as the **New York City Aquarium on 10 December 1896** — the city's first. — NPS, "History & Culture, Castle Clinton" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/index.htm
33. The aquarium **closed in 1941** and relocated to **Coney Island**. — NPS, "History & Culture, Castle Clinton" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/index.htm
34. The city planned to **demolish Castle Clinton** for the construction of the **Brooklyn-Battery Tunnel**; concerned citizens saved it. — NPS, "History & Culture, Castle Clinton" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/index.htm
35. It was **designated a National Monument on 12 August 1946**. — NPS, "Fact Sheet: Castle Clinton National Monument" https://www.nps.gov/npnh/learn/news/fact-sheet-cacl.htm
36. It **reopened to the public in 1975** as Castle Clinton National Monument. — NPS, "History & Culture, Castle Clinton" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/index.htm
37. Today it is the **ferry ticket office** for the Statue of Liberty and Ellis Island, with a museum gallery of dioramas covering **1811–1940**, and free 20-minute ranger tours. This is literally where the listener is standing. — NPS, "Fact Sheet: Castle Clinton National Monument" https://www.nps.gov/npnh/learn/news/fact-sheet-cacl.htm
38. Address of record: **Battery Park, New York, NY 10004**. — NPS, "Fact Sheet: Castle Clinton National Monument" https://www.nps.gov/npnh/learn/news/fact-sheet-cacl.htm

### The WPA Guide, 1939 — the period voice for this place
38a. **Edition, exactly:** *New York City Guide*, American Guide Series, **Random House, 1939**, xx + 680 pp. "*The WPA Guide to New York City*" is the 1982-and-later reprint title — **same text, different pagination.** Page numbers below are the 1939 printing. Scan used: https://archive.org/details/newyorkcityguide00federich
38b. **[RIGHTS FLAG]** The 1939 volume is copyrighted **"BY THE GUILDS' COMMITTEE FOR FEDERAL WRITERS' PUBLICATIONS, INC."** — *not* the US Government. Its public-domain status rests on **non-renewal**, not on the federal-works exclusion. **Run a renewal check before shipping audio that quotes it at length.** Short quotations with attribution are low risk; long passages are not.
38c. **WPA Guide on the fort:** "Built by the Federal Government about 1807, it was known at first as the West Battery and stood on the Capske, a cluster of rocks a short distance from the shore line of that time. It was renamed Castle Clinton after the War of 1812." — *New York City Guide*, 1939, p. 308. **Note "the Capske"** — the name of the rocks this fort stands on. Almost nobody knows it.
38d. **WPA Guide, the best read-aloud passage:** "As Castle Garden it was the scene of notable public and social events. Lafayette was welcomed here in 1824, Louis Kossuth in 1851, and Edward VII—then Prince of Wales—in 1860. Here Professor Morse demonstrated the telegraph in 1835, and in 1850, under the sponsorship of P. T. 38e. **WPA Guide on what survives:** "The original eight-foot-thick walls, massive bolt-studded doors, and gun embrasures of Castle Clinton remain." — *New York City Guide*, 1939, p. 308
38f. **WPA Guide on the Battery:** "The Battery, threshold of Manhattan, spreads in a decided arc along the North River shore at the southernmost extremity of the island, where East and North rivers empty their sediment into the Upper Bay." — *New York City Guide*, 1939, p. 60

### The aquarium years, 1896–1941
38j. **It opened 10 December 1896 as the first ever New York Aquarium.** — NPS, "Aquarium Era 1896–1941" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/aquarium-era.htm
38k. **More than 30,000 visitors came on opening day**, and it averaged over **5,000 people per day**. — NPS, *Foundation Document, Castle Clinton National Monument*, September 2018, p. 3
38l. **The headline number: 84,336,316 visitors** passed through the sally port during the 39 years the New York Zoological Society ran it. — NPS, "Aquarium Era" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/aquarium-era.htm
38m. **The WPA Guide's mid-run figure corroborates it:** "In the thirty-six years ending December, 1938, **more than seventy-six million visitors** passed under the two gilded figures of sea horses carved above the main doorway. The average attendance is about seven thousand a day." — *New York City Guide*, 1939, p. 307. (76M by end-1938 plus three more years lands near 84M — **not a conflict.**)
38n. **The 1939 census of the collection:** "some **8,877 fishes, 872 invertebrates, 198 reptiles, 65 amphibians, and 12 birds.**" — *New York City Guide*, 1939, pp. 307–308
38o. **The crowd-pleasers:** "The **electric eels**, which generate enough current to light a bulb above the tank, are first in popular acclaim; runners-up are the lacy Siamese fighting fish, hideous green and spotted morays, and grotesque toadfish." — *New York City Guide*, 1939, p. 309
38p. **The single strangest fact in this whole document:** "Wireless operators on ocean freighters obligingly carry to far-off corners of the world **castoff clothes, whisky, and other goods** given them by the aquarium, and **barter them for rare fish** to add to the aquarium's collection." — *New York City Guide*, 1939, p. 310. (Verified letter-by-letter: "whisky" with no E, "castoff clothes." The setup clause is what makes the line land.)
38q. **The star of the aquarium was a harbor seal named Nellie**, "who would put on a show for guests, playfully throwing water over those who gathered around her tank." — NPS, "Aquarium Era" https://www.nps.gov/cacl/learn/historyculture/aquarium-era.htm
38r. **Opening-day chaos, quoted by NPS from *The Sun*:** "…unless one was willing to go around two or three times he didn't see much of the fish." — NPS, "Aquarium Era"
38s. Management passed to the **New York Zoological Society on 13 October 1902**, under **Dr. Charles H. Townsend**, former chief of the fisheries division of the U.S. Fish Commission. — NPS, "Aquarium Era"
38t. **McKim, Mead & White rebuilt it 1922–1926**, adding two storeys — and that shell is what was later demolished, with the 1811 fort surviving inside it. — NPS, "Aquarium Era"
38u. **Why it closed — and who closed it:** "The New York Aquarium would run its course until **October 1, 1941** when it was closed to accommodate for the construction of the Brooklyn-Battery tunnel... The decision to close the aquarium was **spearheaded by the bridge's proponent, Robert Moses.**" — NPS, "Aquarium Era"
38v. **A citizen, to the *Brooklyn Eagle*:** "Can't we Brooklynites and business men in lower Manhattan get together and stop him? **If ever there was a landmark in lower Manhattan it is the Aquarium.**" — NPS, "Aquarium Era"
38w. The collection went to the **Bronx Zoo first**; the aquarium reopened at **Coney Island in 1955** — fourteen years later. — NPS, "Aquarium Era"

### The rescue — how the building the family is standing in nearly died
38x. Moses announced a **bridge** in place of the planned tunnel on **22 January 1939**. The **War Department refused approval on 17 July 1939** on navigation and national-defense grounds, affirmed by FDR; Moses reverted to the tunnel. — New York Preservation Archive Project https://www.nypap.org/preservation-history/brooklyn-battery-bridge/ ⚠️ **NYPAP is a preservation nonprofit, not a government source** — the fullest narrative available, flagged as such.
38y. **On 6 February 1941 Moses announced he would demolish Castle Clinton**, claiming tunnel work would undermine it. Manhattan Commissioner of Borough Works **Walter Binger** called the claim **"ridiculous"** and **"absurd."** — NYPAP https://www.nypap.org/preservation-history/castle-clinton/
38z. **Moses called the fort the "red wart"** on lower Manhattan, and argued it had no significance because **"a shot had never been fired" from it.** The very fact that makes it interesting, he used as the argument for knocking it down. — NYPAP
38aa. **George McAneny** (American Scenic and Historic Preservation Society; Regional Plan Association) organised **18 associations** against demolition, obtained Binger's official inspection disproving Moses's claim, and got the *New York Herald Tribune* to **fly a plane over the fenced site for a front-page aerial photograph** showing the walls still standing. — NYPAP
38ab. **Albert S. Bard** headed the **Central Committee of Organizations Opposing the Battery Toll Bridge**. — NYPAP
38ac. **The Fine Arts Federation of New York** ran a design competition in **August 1941** "for the Selection of an Alternative Design for the Development of Battery Park" — proving the park worked with the fort intact. Source document: **Albert S. Bard Papers, New York Public Library.** — NYPAP
38ad. The Board of Estimate approved demolition **11–5**. The roof and the McKim, Mead & White aquarium shell were destroyed — **"all that remained were the fort's original walls,"** hidden behind a fence. — NYPAP
38ae. **NPS confirms the survival:** "the original 1812 fort had been preserved inside the Aquarium walls." — NPS, "Castle Clinton National Monument" https://www.nps.gov/places/castle-clinton-national-monument.htm
38af. **Congress agreed the walls were sound:** "With little effort and small expense **the thick circular red sandstone walls of the original fort, still perfectly intact,** can be brought to view and permanently preserved." — S. Rept. 79-1899, Committee on Public Lands and Surveys, 29 July 1946 https://www.govinfo.gov/app/details/SERIALSET-11017_00_00-291-1899-0000
38ag. **The Act, cited precisely: Public Law 721, 79th Congress, 2d Session; Chapter 954; H.R. 5125; approved 12 August 1946; 60 Stat. 997.** https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-60/pdf/STATUTE-60-Pg997.pdf
38ah. **Verbatim, Section 1:** "…the Secretary of the Interior is authorized to accept, on behalf of the United States, title to the site, comprising approximately one acre and situated in Battery Park, New York City, of the historic structure known as Castle Clinton… **When title to such property is vested in the United States, it shall constitute the Castle Clinton National Monument.**" — 60 Stat. 997
38ai. **THE BEST STRUCTURAL TWIST IN SECTION 1 — and it is fully documented.** **The 1946 Act did not save Castle Clinton.** By its own words it only authorised the Secretary to *accept* title, and the monument would exist only once title vested. **Moses held the property for four more years.** What closed the gap was Albany legislation forcing the cession — after McAneny's inspection report and that *Herald Tribune* aerial photograph publicly destroyed Moses's claim that there was nothing left behind the fence. — 60 Stat. 997; NYPAP
38aj. **Quotable, Senate Report 1946:** "Here Samuel F. B. Morse gave his first demonstration of the telegraph and here Jenny Lind was introduced to America… **Through its gates passed some 8,000,000 future Americans whose grandchildren and great grandchildren have in our day fought to preserve our common national heritage.**" — S. Rept. 79-1899, 29 July 1946. ⚠️ "first demonstration of the telegraph" is a 1946 congressional overstatement — see fact 16t.
38ak. **Title actually transferred in 1950.** — NYC Parks, "The Battery" https://www.nycgovparks.org/parks/battery-park/history ; NPS National Register nomination. ⚠️ **18 July 1950** circulates as the day but **every NPS source says only "1950."** Treat the day as probable, not verified.
38al. **Restoration phase one, with a contract figure:** "The first portion of the restoration was accomplished in **1968 and 1969**... **The cost of this contract was $515,000.**" — Holland & Jones, *Special History Study: Masonry Forts of the National Park Service*, NPS, August 1973, p. 162
38am. **What was undone:** "removal of the aquarium roof; the removal of the stucco from the exterior walls; restored officers' quarters; restored portico and casemate roofs," returning it to its **1811–23** appearance. — NPS, National Register nomination (Millman & Weible, Feb 1983/Mar 1984)
38an. **"Saved from demolition in 1946, Castle Clinton was restored to its original design by the National Park Service. The site reopened in 1975."** — NPS, *Foundation Document, Castle Clinton National Monument*, September 2018, p. 3
38ao. **[UNVERIFIED]** A **24 May 1975** reopening, a Beethoven Ninth, and a **$750,000** cost all circulate in secondary sources. **No NPS, LPC, LOC or NARA source gives a day, a concert, or that figure.** Do not narrate them.
38ap. **Sobering, and worth saying plainly:** "The only elements remaining of the original structure are **select pieces of the foundation and sandstone walls.**" Most of what the family can touch is reconstruction. — NPS, *Foundation Document*, September 2018, p. 6

**The structural irony for the script:** the building the family is queuing at was the *previous* front door. Eight million people walked through it before the statue in front of them was finished, and before Ellis Island opened. Every stage of the arrival story — fort, concert hall, landing depot, aquarium, ticket window — happened in the same circle of stone.

## 2. The idea and the making of the statue

### The idea
39. **Édouard de Laboulaye proposed the statue in 1865.** He was a French political intellectual and anti-slavery activist. — NPS, "Creating the Statue of Liberty" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/places_creating_statue.htm
40. Laboulaye proposed "Liberty Enlightening the World" to commemorate **the Union's 1865 Civil War victory and the abolition of slavery**. — NPS, "Abolition" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/abolition.htm
41. He **co-founded and presided over the French Anti-Slavery Society** (established 1865), and was an honorary member of the Philadelphia Union League Club. — NPS, "Abolition" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/abolition.htm
42. The idea was floated at a **dinner Laboulaye hosted near Versailles in 1865**, with Bartholdi — then a young Alsatian sculptor — among the guests. — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954), ch. "An Idea Is Born" https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11b.htm
43. **Quotable, Laboulaye:** "Until a new sort of politics was lately found for us, it was accepted on both sides of the ocean as a virtual article of faith that America and France are sisters." — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11b.htm
44. Laboulaye's argument was that nations remember **"the names and deeds of individuals who came to their aid"** in a crisis. — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11b.htm

### Bartholdi
45. **Frédéric-Auguste Bartholdi, born 1834 in Colmar**, Alsace, near the German border; **died 1904**. — NPS, "Frédéric-Auguste Bartholdi" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/fr%C3%A9d%C3%A9ric-auguste-bartholdi.htm
46. His first commission, at **age twenty**, was a bronze of the Napoleonic general **Jean Rapp**. — NPS, "Frédéric-Auguste Bartholdi" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/fr%C3%A9d%C3%A9ric-auguste-bartholdi.htm
47. **The Egypt rehearsal:** Bartholdi travelled to Egypt and proposed a colossal lighthouse to Khedive Isma'il Pasha, who oversaw the Suez Canal — a robed Egyptian woman titled **"Egypt (or Progress) Carrying the Light to Asia."** **The khedive rejected it.** — NPS, "Frédéric-Auguste Bartholdi" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/fr%C3%A9d%C3%A9ric-auguste-bartholdi.htm
48. He **began designing "Liberty Enlightening the World" in 1870**. — NPS, "Creating the Statue of Liberty" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/places_creating_statue.htm
49. He sailed to America in **1871** carrying letters of introduction from Laboulaye, and within three months had secured **Bedloe's Island** as the site, seeing it as **"the gateway to America."** — NPS, "Frédéric-Auguste Bartholdi" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/fr%C3%A9d%C3%A9ric-auguste-bartholdi.htm ; NPS, "Creating the Statue of Liberty" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/places_creating_statue.htm
50. The 1954 handbook notes he conceived the monument on the ocean voyage, but **"the plan did not actually crystallize until he saw New York Harbor."** — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11b.htm
51. **Quotable, Bartholdi:** "My dream has been realized. I can only say that I am enchanted. This thing will live to eternity, when we shall have passed away, and everything living with us has moldered away." — NPS, "Frédéric-Auguste Bartholdi" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/fr%C3%A9d%C3%A9ric-auguste-bartholdi.htm
52. **Quotable, Bartholdi (December 1882, from Paris):** "the Statue commences to reach above the houses, and by next spring we shall see it over-look the entire city." — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11h.htm
53. Bartholdi modelled the figure on the **Roman goddess Libertas**, after many rejected iterations. — NPS, "Statue of Liberty Pedestal and Museum Self-Guided Tour" (PDF) https://www.nps.gov/common/uploads/teachers/lessonplans/PedestalMuseum%20Self-Guided%20Tour.pdf

### The symbolism
54. **The broken chains.** A broken shackle and chain lie at her feet: the shackle in front of her **right foot**, the chain running beneath the drapery and reappearing at the **left foot, the end link modelled as broken.** — NPS, "Abolition" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/abolition.htm
55. She is shown **free from oppression and servitude**, reflecting the recent abolition of slavery. — NPS, "Abolition" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/abolition.htm
56. **You cannot see the chains from the ground or from the ferry** — they are largely hidden by the drapery and by her height. That is a genuinely good "look again" beat: the abolition symbolism is the part almost nobody sees.
57. **Her right foot is raised** — she is walking forward, "progress is a journey, a never-ending movement forward." — NPS, "Statue of Liberty Facts" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/statue-of-liberty-facts.htm
58. **Seven rays** on the crown, representing **the seven continents and the seven seas**. — NPS, "Statue of Liberty Facts" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/statue-of-liberty-facts.htm
59. The crown also has **25 windows**. — NPS, "Statue Statistics" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/statue-statistics.htm
60. **The tablet in her left arm reads "JULY IV MDCCLXXVI"** — 4 July 1776. — NPS, "Statue Statistics" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/statue-statistics.htm
61. The tablet is **23 ft 7 in long, 13 ft 7 in wide, and 2 ft thick**. — NPS, "Statue Statistics" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/statue-statistics.htm ; NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11k1.htm
62. Her official name is **"La Liberté éclairant le monde" — "Liberty Enlightening the World."** — NPS, "Statue of Liberty Facts" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/statue-of-liberty-facts.htm
63. **The complicated legacy, stated by NPS itself:** despite the abolitionist symbolism, African Americans faced persistent racism after 1886, and for many the monument represented **broken promises rather than achieved freedom.** — NPS, "Abolition" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/abolition.htm

### Repoussé and the skin
64. **Repoussé** is "the process of molding lightweight copper sheets by hammering them onto the Statue's hallowed wooden framework" — a technique developed for this purpose by **Eugène Viollet-le-Duc**. — NPS, "Creating the Statue of Liberty" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/places_creating_statue.htm
65. **More than 300 separate sheets of copper**, each hand-hammered over an individual mould, form the envelope of the statue. — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11e.htm
66. **The copper skin is 3/32 of an inch thick (about 2.5 mm) — "the same as two American pennies placed together."** This is the single best physical fact in the whole chapter: a 305-foot statue with a skin the thickness of two coins. — NPS, "Frequently Asked Questions" https://www.nps.gov/stli/faqs.htm
67. The scaling method: from the first model, a **2.85-metre** statue was reproduced; from that, a model four times larger at **11 metres (36 feet)**. — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11e.htm
68. **For each section of the enlarged model, about 9,000 separate measurements** had to be taken. — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11e.htm

### Eiffel's framework — and why she moves
69. **Eugène Viollet-le-Duc** designed the supporting metal framework first, but **died unexpectedly in 1879**; the Franco-American Union and Bartholdi hired **Alexandre-Gustave Eiffel** as his replacement. — NPS, "Alexandre-Gustave Eiffel" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/alexandre-gustave-eiffel.htm
70. **Eiffel: born 15 December 1832 in Dijon, died 27 December 1923 in Paris.** He made his name on railway bridges "renowned for their lightness, grace, and strength." — NPS, "Alexandre-Gustave Eiffel" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/alexandre-gustave-eiffel.htm
71. Eiffel designed a **central pylon 92 feet (28 m) tall** as the primary interior support. — NPS, "Alexandre-Gustave Eiffel" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/alexandre-gustave-eiffel.htm
72. The pylon connects to **"a lightweight truss work of complex asymmetrical girders"** forming the body. — NPS, "Alexandre-Gustave Eiffel" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/alexandre-gustave-eiffel.htm
73. **The mechanism — this is the key sentence:** "Flat metal bars are bolted at one end to the pylon and to the copper skin at the other end," creating a **"flexible suspension (due to their malleability), acting like springs allowing the Statue to adjust."** — NPS, "Alexandre-Gustave Eiffel" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/alexandre-gustave-eiffel.htm
74. Why it matters: she must withstand **harbour winds, temperature changes and weather** — Eiffel solved it with flexibility rather than mass. — NPS, "Alexandre-Gustave Eiffel" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/alexandre-gustave-eiffel.htm
75. **The 1954 handbook's version, and it is vivid:** "Four huge iron posts run from the base of the statue to the top, forming a pylon which bears the weight of the whole structure," and **"each section is supported independently—no plate of copper hangs from the one above it or bears upon the one below."** — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11e.htm
76. **She sways up to 3 inches; the torch sways up to 6 inches.** — NPS, "Statue Statistics" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/statue-statistics.htm
77. NPS describes this as a **"flexible curtain-wall type of construction that supports the skin"** — cited as part of her World Heritage significance, i.e. a direct ancestor of modern skyscraper cladding. — NPS, "Statue of Liberty: World Heritage Site" https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/statue-of-liberty-national-monument-world-heritage-site.htm
78. The armature bars **"help the skin of the statue to bend and flex in the wind and weather."** — NPS, "Statue of Liberty Pedestal and Museum Self-Guided Tour" (PDF) https://www.nps.gov/common/uploads/teachers/lessonplans/PedestalMuseum%20Self-Guided%20Tour.pdf

### Built in pieces, shown in pieces
79. **The arm and torch were completed in 1876 and shown at the Centennial Exposition in Philadelphia.** — NPS, "Creating the Statue of Liberty" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/places_creating_statue.htm
80. The arm and torch **later stood in Madison Square Park, New York, before being shipped back to France.** — NPS, "Statue of Liberty Pedestal and Museum Self-Guided Tour" (PDF) https://www.nps.gov/common/uploads/teachers/lessonplans/PedestalMuseum%20Self-Guided%20Tour.pdf
81. **[UNVERIFIED]** The widely repeated detail that visitors paid **50 cents** to climb a ladder to the torch balcony, with proceeds going to the pedestal fund, could **not** be confirmed on an NPS or LOC page in this research. The 1954 NPS handbook records the Philadelphia and Madison Square displays but **mentions no admission fee**. Do not narrate the fee as fact without a citation. — checked against NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11c.htm
81a. What IS citable: **"the completed right arm and torch of the statue [were displayed] at the Centennial Exposition in Philadelphia in 1876 and later at Madison Square in New York City."** — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11c.htm
81b. **Period image, free to use:** "Colossal hand and torch, 'Liberty'" — Library of Congress Prints & Photographs https://www.loc.gov/item/97502738/ . This is what Philadelphia crowds actually saw in 1876: a forearm the height of a house, standing on grass.
82. **The head and shoulders were finished in 1878 and displayed at the Paris Universal Exposition.** — NPS, "Creating the Statue of Liberty" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/places_creating_statue.htm
83. The statue was **assembled entire in Paris between 1881 and 1884**, standing over the rooftops of the city. — NPS, "Creating the Statue of Liberty" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/places_creating_statue.htm
84. It could have been finished in **1883**, "but since the pedestal was not ready for it, work on the statue was slowed a bit." — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11h.htm
85. A **banquet on 11 June 1884** celebrated the statue's completion. — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11h.htm
86. **On 4 July 1884**, Count **Ferdinand de Lesseps** formally presented the statue to the United States through Minister **Levi P. Morton**. — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11h.htm ; NPS, "Creating the Statue of Liberty" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/places_creating_statue.htm
87. **Quotable, de Lesseps, 4 July 1884:** "We now transfer to you, Mr. Minister, this great statue and trust that it may forever stand the pledge of friendship between France and the Great Republic." — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11h.htm

### The crossing
88. For the voyage the statue was **reduced to 350 individual pieces and packed in 214 crates.** — Statue of Liberty–Ellis Island Foundation, "Overview + History" https://www.statueofliberty.org/statue-of-liberty/overview-history/
89. The 1954 NPS handbook gives **"214 specially constructed cases which, when filled, varied in weight from a few hundred pounds to several tons."** — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11j.htm
90. The cases were **"marked so that it could be reassembled on Bedloe's Island with accuracy and efficiency"** — a 350-piece flat-pack. — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11j.htm
91. The French navy frigate **Isère departed Rouen on 21 May 1885** and **arrived at Sandy Hook, at the entrance of New York Harbor, on 17 June**. — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11j.htm
92. NPS elsewhere gives the arrival in New York Harbor as **17 June 1885**; the 1954 handbook notes the statue reached **Bedloe's Island on 19 June 1885**. Both are consistent (harbour entrance, then island). — NPS, "Creating the Statue of Liberty" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/places_creating_statue.htm ; NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11d.htm
93. **The statue arrived with nowhere to stand.** She sat in crates on the island while the pedestal money was still being raised — the dramatic hinge into section 3. — NPS, "Pulitzer- In Depth" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/pulitzer-in-depth.htm
94. **Dedicated 28 October 1886.** — NPS, "Creating the Statue of Liberty" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/places_creating_statue.htm

## 3. The pedestal and the money

### Hunt
115. **Richard Morris Hunt, born 31 October 1828 in Brattleboro, Vermont; died 31 July 1895 in Newport, Rhode Island.** — NPS, "Richard Morris Hunt" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/richard-morris-hunt.htm
116. He was **"the first American to attend the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris."** — NPS, "Richard Morris Hunt" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/richard-morris-hunt.htm
117. He **founded the American Institute of Architects in 1857** and was its first secretary. He also designed the New York Tribune Building and the Lenox Library. — NPS, "Richard Morris Hunt" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/richard-morris-hunt.htm
118. The pedestal took **two years of collaboration with Bartholdi** and **numerous rejected proposals by both men** before the final 1884 design. — NPS, "Richard Morris Hunt" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/richard-morris-hunt.htm
119. Hunt's **"Pharos I"** concept merged Bartholdi's fortress idea with the ancient **lighthouse at Alexandria** — one of the seven wonders, answering a colossus with a colossus. — NPS, "Richard Morris Hunt" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/richard-morris-hunt.htm
120. **Money forced him to shrink it: from 114 feet (34.8 m) down to 87 feet (27.4 m).** — NPS, "Richard Morris Hunt" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/richard-morris-hunt.htm
121. **[CONFLICT — pedestal height]** The Hunt page says the design was cut to **87 ft**; the 1954 handbook says the finished pedestal stands **"89 feet above its foundation"** and the dimensions table gives granite pedestal **89 ft** + foundation **65 ft** = the 154 ft "ground to pedestal" figure. Use **89 ft as built**, 114→87 ft as the design cut. — https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11g.htm ; https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11k1.htm
122. **Hunt was paid $1,000 — and donated it straight back to the American Committee.** — NPS, "Richard Morris Hunt" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/richard-morris-hunt.htm

### The site and the build
123. In **1877 Congress authorized the site**, and **General Sherman** selected **Bedloe's Island**, as Bartholdi wanted. **General Charles P. Stone** was engineer in chief. — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11g.htm
124. The pedestal was set **inside the existing walls of Fort Wood** — an eleven-pointed star fort. — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11g.htm
125. **Ground was broken for the foundation on 18 April 1883**; digging was hard going because of the fort's masonry. — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11g.htm
126. The foundation is **almost solid concrete, placed to a depth of 20 feet**. — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11g.htm
127. The concrete foundation weighs **54,000,000 lbs**. — NPS, "Statue Statistics" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/statue-statistics.htm
128. The outer wall is **granite from Leete's Island, Connecticut**, backed by a massive shaft of concrete. — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11g.htm
129. **The cornerstone was laid on 5 August 1884** by **William A. Brodie, Grand Master of the Grand Lodge of New York State**, with Masonic ceremony. — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11g.htm
130. **Work stopped in the autumn of 1884 with "only 15 feet of the structure completed"** — the money had run out. — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11g.htm
131. **A lovely closing detail:** "On April 22, 1886, the last stone of the pedestal was swung into place and the jubilant workmen showered into the mortar a collection of silver coins from their own pockets." — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11g.htm

### Who paid for what — the split that explains everything
131a. **The deal was: France pays for the statue, America pays for the pedestal.** That division is the reason for the crisis.
131b. **The statue's final cost was approximately $250,000** — and **"Every penny of this fund was contributed by the French people."** — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11c.htm
131c. **The French subscription was not completed until July 1882.** — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11c.htm
131d. **How France raised it:** "Public fetes and other entertainments were given to help raise funds"; **Charles Gounod wrote a song presented at the Paris Opera**; and **"it was decided to organize a lottery to augment the fund."** — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11c.htm
131e. **The American side's job was costed at $125,000:** "the cost of constructing the pedestal and placing the statue upon it would be $125,000." — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11c.htm
131f. **The comparison to say aloud:** France raised a quarter of a million dollars by public subscription, lottery and opera galas. America was asked for half that, for a plinth, and could not do it. That is the shame Pulitzer weaponised.

### The failure, then Pulitzer
131g. **"Appeals were made in vain to the people, to the New York Assembly, and to the Congress of the United States."** All three said no. — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11c.htm
131h. By **January 1885 "the committee again found itself without a penny in its treasury."** The statue was crated on the dock five months later. — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11c.htm
132. In **September 1876** a committee was appointed by the **Union League Club** in New York, with **John Jay** as chairman, to raise pedestal funds — **"public apathy continued, and the necessary funds were not obtained."** — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11c.htm
133. **Joseph Pulitzer became owner and editor of the New York World in 1883** and took up the pedestal campaign. — NPS, "Joseph Pulitzer" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/joseph-pulitzer.htm
134. **"Then, on March 16, 1885, the World renewed its pedestal fund campaign in grim earnest."** — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11d.htm
135. Pulitzer's argument was that the statue was **"a gift of the whole people of France to the whole people of America"** — not a transaction between rich men. — NPS, "Pulitzer- In Depth" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/pulitzer-in-depth.htm
136. He **stormed at wealthy men who would not pay, and equally at ordinary citizens content to let the rich do it.** — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11d.htm
137. **The mechanism that worked: he promised to print the name of every single donor in the paper, no matter how small the sum** — on the front page. — NPS, "Pulitzer- In Depth" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/pulitzer-in-depth.htm
138. He ran **human-interest stories, including children saving up to give**. — NPS, "Pulitzer- In Depth" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/pulitzer-in-depth.htm
139. **"School children were appealed to, and their contributions appreciably aided the fund."** — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11d.htm
140. **The result: over $100,000 from more than 120,000 donors.** — NPS, "Pulitzer- In Depth" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/pulitzer-in-depth.htm
141. **The arithmetic to say aloud: that is an average of well under a dollar a person.** The pedestal under the statue was bought, in effect, in small change. (Derived from fact 140 — present as arithmetic, not as a sourced figure.)
142. **"By August 11, less than 5 months after it had launched its latest fund drive, the World was able to announce that the pedestal fund had been completed"** — and the placing of the statue on Bedloe's Island was assured. — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11d.htm
143. **Quotable, Pulitzer:** "Publicity, publicity, publicity is the greatest moral factor and force in our public life." — NPS, "Pulitzer- In Depth" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/pulitzer-in-depth.htm

## 4. Emma Lazarus and "The New Colossus"

### Her
146. **Emma Lazarus, born 22 July 1849 in New York City; died 19 November 1887**, probably of Hodgkin's lymphoma; buried in Queens. She was **38**. — NPS, "Emma Lazarus" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/emma-lazarus.htm
147. She came from a **wealthy Portuguese Sephardic Jewish sugar-refining family.** — NPS, "Emma Lazarus" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/emma-lazarus.htm
148. **She worked as an aide to Jewish refugees** detained on Ward's Island — she had met the people in the poem. — NPS, "Emma Lazarus" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/emma-lazarus.htm
149. NPS names the three forces behind the sonnet: **"her Sephardic Jewish heritage, her experiences working with refugees on Ward's Island, and the plight of the immigrant."** — NPS, "Emma Lazarus" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/emma-lazarus.htm

### The poem
150. **It was written for money — to raise it.** **William Maxwell Evarts** and the author **Constance Cary Harrison** asked her for a sonnet for the **"Art Loan Fund Exhibition in Aid of the Bartholdi Pedestal Fund."** — NPS, "Emma Lazarus" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/emma-lazarus.htm
151. **She composed "The New Colossus" on 2 November 1883**, for that charity auction. — NPS, "Emma Lazarus" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/emma-lazarus.htm ; NPS, "The New Colossus" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/colossus.htm
152. The poem then appeared in **Joseph Pulitzer's New York World and in The New York Times** — the poem and the newspaper campaign are the same story. — NPS, "Emma Lazarus" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/emma-lazarus.htm
153. The sonnet named the statue **"Mother of Exiles."** That phrase, not "Statue of Liberty," is where the immigration meaning is born. — NPS, "Emma Lazarus" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/emma-lazarus.htm
154. **She never heard it read at the statue.** She died in 1887, and after her death **public memory of the poem faded.** — NPS, "Emma Lazarus" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/emma-lazarus.htm
155. **In 1901 her friend Georgina Schuyler rediscovered the poem** and organised the effort to resurrect it. — NPS, "Emma Lazarus" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/emma-lazarus.htm
156. **In 1903 the words were cast on a bronze plaque and placed inside the pedestal** — **seventeen years after the statue was dedicated, twenty years after the poem was written, sixteen years after Lazarus died.** — NPS, "The New Colossus" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/colossus.htm ; NPS, "Emma Lazarus" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/emma-lazarus.htm
157. **An exact replica of that plaque is now in the Statue of Liberty Museum.** — NPS, "The New Colossus" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/colossus.htm
158. NPS states the statue's significance **expanded** after the 1903 plaque, "transforming it into an immigration symbol." — Statue of Liberty–Ellis Island Foundation, "Overview + History" https://www.statueofliberty.org/statue-of-liberty/overview-history/
159. **The gap to narrate:** Laboulaye and Bartholdi built a monument to **republican liberty and the end of slavery** (facts 40–41, 54–55). The **immigration** meaning is retrofitted — by a poet in 1883, by a friend in 1901, by a plaque in 1903, and finally by millions of people who sailed past her. The statue did not greet the Castle Garden generation with that meaning; it acquired it from them.

### The text — public domain, exact
160. **"The New Colossus" (1883) is public domain** (published 1883; Lazarus died 1887). Text below transcribed exactly from the NPS teaching copy. — NPS, "The New Colossus by Emma Lazarus" (PDF) https://www.nps.gov/common/uploads/teachers/lessonplans/The%20New%20Colossus-%20Emma%20Lazarus.pdf

> **The New Colossus**
> by Emma Lazarus
>
> Not like the brazen giant of Greek fame,
> With conquering limbs astride from land to land;
> Here at our sea-washed, sunset gates shall stand
> A mighty woman with a torch, whose flame
> Is the imprisoned lightning, and her name
> Mother of Exiles. From her beacon-hand
> Glows world-wide welcome; her mild eyes command
> The air-bridged harbor that twin cities frame.
> "Keep ancient lands, your storied pomp!" cries she
> With silent lips. "Give me your tired, your poor,
> Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free,
> The wretched refuse of your teeming shore.
> Send these, the homeless, tempest-tost to me,

161. **The famous lines, for the script:** *"Give me your tired, your poor, / Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free, / The wretched refuse of your teeming shore. / Send these, the homeless, tempest-tost to me, / I lift my lamp beside the golden door!"* — same source as 160.
162. **Two under-quoted lines worth using instead**, because everyone knows the famous ones: *"A mighty woman with a torch, whose flame / Is the imprisoned lightning"* — she is describing **electric light**, brand new in 1883, and it connects straight to the lighthouse story in section 6.
163. **"The air-bridged harbor that twin cities frame"** — the twin cities are **New York and Brooklyn**, separate cities until 1898, bridged in 1883, the year she wrote it. A detail a well-read visitor usually misses.
164. **Spelling note for the voice artist:** the NPS text reads **"tempest-tost"**, not "tempest-tossed."

## 5. What arriving immigrants actually saw — and Ellis Island

### The sequence — and the correction that matters
165. **The sequence is usually told wrong, including in most audio tours.** The correct order is: **statue sighted from the deck → quarantine inspection handled separately at Hoffman and Swinburne Islands off Staten Island → first- and second-class passengers inspected on board and released at the Manhattan pier → steerage passengers ferried to Ellis Island.**
166. **Quarantine was NOT at Ellis Island.** "Ellis Island's doctors were not involved with quarantine – this operation took place on Hoffman and Swinburne Islands, two isolated islands off the coast of Staten Island." — NPS, "Doctor" https://www.nps.gov/elis/learn/historyculture/people_doctor.htm
167. **The class split — the single most important fact in this section.** "Third class and 'steerage' immigrants, coming through the Port of New York, were the largest group processed at Ellis Island. First and second-class immigrant travelers were processed on-board steamships entering New York Harbor due to their higher financial status and lower risk of carrying dangerous diseases or having other severe health issues." — NPS, Ellis Island FAQs https://www.nps.gov/elis/faqs.htm
168. Put plainly: **"Once immigrant steamships docked in New York Harbor, first and second class passengers disembarked while the 'steerage' passengers were transferred to Ellis Island by ferries and barges."** **Ellis Island was not for everyone. It was for the poor.** — NPS, Ellis Island FAQs https://www.nps.gov/elis/faqs.htm
169. **Everyone saw the statue. Only some went to Ellis Island.** That asymmetry is the emotional spine of this section — the statue was the universal welcome; the inspection was means-tested.

### First-hand accounts — fully public domain, freely quotable
170. **Stephen Graham, *With Poor Immigrants to America* (New York: Macmillan, 1914)** — a writer who crossed in steerage himself. Published pre-1929 in the US, therefore **public domain**. Library of Congress record: https://www.loc.gov/item/14015813/
171. **Quotable, Graham 1914 — the first sight:** "At length we came into sight of the green-grey statue of Liberty, far away and diminutive at first, but later on, a celestial figure in a blaze of sunlight." — Graham, *With Poor Immigrants to America*, 1914. **Note "green-grey" — by 1914 she had turned. See fact 196.**
172. **Quotable, Graham 1914 — THE BEST LINE IN THE WHOLE FACT SHEET:** "An American waved a starry flag in greeting, and some emigrants were disposed to cheer, some shed silent tears. Many, however, did not know what the statue was. **I heard one Russian telling another that it was the tombstone of Columbus.**" — Graham, 1914. First-hand, public domain, and it puncture the myth without cynicism: not everyone arrived knowing what she was.
173. **Graham's timetable of a landing day, minute by minute** (pp. 42–43): the ship lifted anchor at 7 a.m. and "glided up the still waters of the harbour"; passengers disembarked at 8:30 to the Customs Wharf, were "quick-marched" to a ferry, waited 45 minutes, and **"At a quarter to ten we steamed for Ellis Island."** — Graham, 1914
174. **Edward A. Steiner, *On the Trail of the Immigrant* (New York: Fleming H. Revell, 1906)** — travelled steerage repeatedly to document the crossing. Pre-1929 US publication, **public domain**. Cited in LOC's immigration bibliography https://catdir.loc.gov/catdir/toc/becites/genealogy/immigrant/70137989.refs.html
175a. **Quotable, Steiner 1906 — the silence:** "The steerage is still mute; it looks to the left at the populous shore, to the right at the green stretches of Long Island, and again straight ahead at the mighty city." — Steiner, 1906
176a. **Quotable, Steiner 1906 — the moment the silence breaks:** "Slowly the ship glides into the harbour, and when it passes under the shadow of the Statue of Liberty, the silence is broken, and **a thousand hands are outstretched in greeting to this new divinity into whose keeping they now entrust themselves.**" — Steiner, 1906. This is the one to end a section on.
177a. **NPS's own framing (US Government work, free to use):** "The Statue of Liberty was a reassuring sign that they had arrived in the land of their dreams. To these anxious newcomers, **the Statue's uplifted torch did not suggest 'enlightenment,' as her creators intended, but rather, 'welcome.'**" — NPS, "The Immigrant's Statue" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/the-immigrants-statue.htm
178e. **"Between 1886 and 1924, almost 14 million immigrants entered the United States through New York."** — NPS, "The Immigrant's Statue" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/the-immigrants-statue.htm

### The one NPS oral history that mentions the statue — RIGHTS-FLAGGED
179a. **Morris Abraham Schneider**, born 3 May 1910, emigrated from Poland in 1920 aged 10 aboard the SS *Rotterdam*; interviewed 17 November 1991, Ellis Island Oral History Collection EI-116. — NPS, "Morris Remembers the Steamship" https://www.nps.gov/elis/learn/education/oral-history-ei-116.htm

### Castle Garden's own voices

### Castle Garden before Ellis — the contrast to narrate
189b. "Castle Garden (originally and presently known as Castle Clinton) was a fort built for the War of 1812, located in Battery Park, NY. It served as the New York immigration processing station between 1855 and 1890." Before Ellis, **"immigrants were required to be processed by the State, not the federal government."** — NPS, Ellis Island FAQs https://www.nps.gov/elis/faqs.htm
190a. **The US Customs Service supervised arrivals at Castle Garden from 1820 until the Office of Superintendent of Immigration was created in 1891.** — National Archives, *Immigrants Arriving at the Port of New York: Records of the U.S. Customs Service, 1820–1897*, Reference Report #800 https://www.archives.gov/files/research/immigration/port/nyc-1820-1897.pdf
191a. **[SUPERSEDED — see fact 30.]** The NPS web pages split between 30 April and 18 April 1890. The NPS monograph devoted to the subject (Svejda 1968) documents **18 April 1890**, names the last two ships and the passenger count, and the date checks out as a Friday. **Use 18 April 1890.**
192a. **The line the whole chapter is built for:** of the **8.28 million** people who came through the building the family is standing at, **the overwhelming majority arrived before the Statue of Liberty existed.** Castle Garden opened in 1855. The statue was dedicated in 1886. **For thirty-one of Castle Garden's thirty-five years, there was no statue in the harbour at all** — just this round stone fort, and a roar you could not understand.
193a. **[UNVERIFIED — soften it]** The widely repeated claim that arrivals were processed at the **Barge Office** during the 1890–1892 gap could **not** be confirmed on NPS, NARA or LOC. NPS documents the Barge Office **only** as the post-fire fallback from June 1897. Say "in the two years between" rather than naming the Barge Office.

### The inspection
210a. **The six-second physical — CONFIRMED by NPS:** "U.S. Public Health Service doctors sometimes had only six seconds to scan each immigrant during the line inspection, a rapid, efficient process meant only to detect those who should be held for a more thorough medical examination." — NPS, "Historic Medical Inspection" https://www.nps.gov/places/000/historic-medical-inspection-2nd-floor.htm
211a. **"In 1917, the U.S. Public Health Service printed a list of over 60 health conditions – from anemia to varicose veins – that doctors could spot during the brief line inspection."** — NPS, "Historic Medical Inspection" https://www.nps.gov/places/000/historic-medical-inspection-2nd-floor.htm
212a. **The staircase was part of the exam and nobody knew it:** "As immigrants entered the building and climbed the stairs, doctors watched for a limp, labored breathing, or other suspected troubles." — NPS, "Historic Medical Inspection" https://www.nps.gov/places/000/historic-medical-inspection-2nd-floor.htm
213a. **The chalk marks — CONFIRMED, with letters:** "Any immigrant suspected of being in questionable health was chalk-marked with a letter of the alphabet (**'B' for back problems, 'F' for face, 'H' for heart**) and taken out of line and moved to a physical or mental examination room." — NPS, "Doctor" https://www.nps.gov/elis/learn/historyculture/people_doctor.htm
214a. **Doctors did not decide who got in:** "Doctors played no role in deciding the fitness of a person to enter the country. This decision was left exclusively in the hands of the U.S. Immigrant Inspectors." — NPS, "Doctor" https://www.nps.gov/elis/learn/historyculture/people_doctor.htm
215a. **The legal inspection:** inspectors "asked immigrants their name, home town, occupation, destination, and the amount of money they carried" — NPS elsewhere states **29 questions**. — NPS, "Registry Room – Great Hall" https://www.nps.gov/places/000/registry-room-great-hall-2nd-floor.htm
216a. **[CORRECTED — the rejection rate is ONE percent, not two.]** NPS states it twice: **"While ten percent were held back for additional medical examination, eventually all but one percent were allowed entry to the United States"**, and "approximately one percent were denied admission for violating American immigration laws." **Say "about one in a hundred."** — NPS, "Historic Medical Inspection" https://www.nps.gov/places/000/historic-medical-inspection-2nd-floor.htm ; NPS, "Registry Room – Great Hall" https://www.nps.gov/places/000/registry-room-great-hall-2nd-floor.htm
217a. **[UNVERIFIED — do NOT say "buttonhook".]** The famous buttonhook used to flip eyelids for trachoma screening is **not named anywhere on nps.gov, loc.gov or archives.gov.** NPS documents the eye exam and trachoma screening but never names the instrument. You may **describe** the eyelid being turned back, and you may use the LOC photograph "U.S. inspectors examining eyes of immigrants, Ellis Island, New York Harbor" https://www.loc.gov/item/97501532/ — but do not narrate "buttonhook" as a sourced fact.
218a. **[UNVERIFIED — do NOT use the peak-day number.]** The widely repeated **11,747 on 17 April 1907** appears on no NPS, LOC or NARA page. The only day-figures NPS publishes are **2,251 (17 Dec 1900)** and **"over 5,000 on busy days."** Use one of those.
219a. **Ellis Island became part of Statue of Liberty National Monument on 11 May 1965** — which is why one ferry ticket covers both, and why the queue the family is in serves both islands. — NPS, "Fact Sheet: Ellis Island" https://www.nps.gov/npnh/learn/news/fact-sheet-elis.htm

## 6. Surprising, checkable details

### She was a lighthouse — and a bad one
170. **On 10 October 1886 — before the dedication — President Grover Cleveland placed the Statue and pedestal under the administration of the U.S. Lighthouse Board, as maritime structures.** — NPS, "Liberty Island Chronology" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/liberty-island-a-chronology.htm
171. **Her torch was electrified for use as a lighthouse in 1886.** — NPS, "Changing Landscapes" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/places_changing_landscapes.htm
172. **She was an official aid to navigation for roughly sixteen years, 1886–1902.** — NPS, "Liberty Island Chronology" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/liberty-island-a-chronology.htm
173. **She was expected to shine 50 miles out to sea. She did not.** After the dedication-night rain cleared, **"it was apparent that Freedom's lamp would not shine out the expected 50 miles to sea; and the lighting problem was long a perplexing one."** — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11k.htm
174. **In 1901 control passed from the Lighthouse Board to the U.S. War Department** — NPS, "Liberty Island Chronology" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/liberty-island-a-chronology.htm ; NPS, "Changing Landscapes" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/places_changing_landscapes.htm
175. **VERIFIED — and this is the best line in the section.** "The Lighthouse Board of the Treasury Department first took jurisdiction over the statue since some considered it an aid to navigation." Then, in 1901, **Secretary of the Treasury Lyman J. Gage** determined that **"the monument had no value as a navigational light and was a financial burden on his department."** — NPS, *Celebrating the Immigrant: An Administrative History of the Statue of Liberty National Monument, 1952–1982* https://npshistory.com/publications/stli/adhi/intro.htm (NPS, page dated 2001)
175a. Gage **"recommended transferring its care to the War Department, which had continued to maintain Fort Wood whose walls surrounded the pedestal,"** and **"the new arrangement was approved by President Theodore Roosevelt in 1901."** — NPS, *Celebrating the Immigrant* https://npshistory.com/publications/stli/adhi/intro.htm
176. **The punchline to narrate:** the most famous lighthouse in the world was quietly taken off the lighthouse list because the light was not good enough to be useful. She was a monument pretending to be infrastructure.
177. **In 1916 she finally got a lighting system that worked** — **President Woodrow Wilson switched it on from the presidential yacht Mayflower, anchored off Bedloe's Island, by pressing a wireless key.** — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11k.htm
178. NPS's chronology records **1916** as the year "The Statue of Liberty is illuminated for the first time" — i.e. the first *adequate* illumination. — NPS, "Liberty Island Chronology" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/liberty-island-a-chronology.htm
178a. **The 1916 system, in numbers:** "Two hundred and forty-six projectors, utilizing 250-watt incandescent lamps." — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11k.htm
178b. "The original electrical system has several times been replaced by more modern equipment." — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11k.htm
178c. **The elevator was an afterthought that took 22 years.** "Although provision was made for an elevator when the pedestal was constructed, the first one was not installed until 1908-9." — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11k.htm
178d. A **1937 inspection** led to replacement of platforms and stairway, and **"Supporting ironwork was replaced where sections of it had rusted"** — the rust problem that would define the 1980s restoration was already visible in 1937. — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11k.htm
179. **Her torch was extinguished 1942–1945** under wartime blackout regulations. — NPS, "Liberty Island Chronology" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/liberty-island-a-chronology.htm

### The torch was cut to pieces
180. **Bartholdi's original torch was solid copper, designed to gleam in daylight — not to be lit from inside.** — Statue of Liberty–Ellis Island Foundation, "Overview + History" https://www.statueofliberty.org/statue-of-liberty/overview-history/
181. **By the 1886 dedication, two rows of portholes had already been cut out of the copper at the bottom of the torch** so it could be lit from within. They started cutting her before she was even unveiled. — Statue of Liberty–Ellis Island Foundation, "Overview + History" https://www.statueofliberty.org/statue-of-liberty/overview-history/
182. **In 1916 the torch was cut open and glazed.** The 1954 NPS handbook: **"the torch, which was originally copper, was redesigned by using a total of 600 separate pieces of various tinted yellow cathedral glass to enhance and beautify the lighting effects. This work was executed by Gutzon Borglum."** — NPS Handbook No. 11 (1954) https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11k.htm
183. **Gutzon Borglum** is the sculptor who went on to carve **Mount Rushmore**. — NPS, Mount Rushmore, "Sculptor Gutzon Borglum" https://www.nps.gov/moru/learn/historyculture/gutzon-borglum.htm
184. **[CONFLICT — number of glass panes]** The 1954 NPS handbook says **600 separate pieces** of tinted yellow cathedral glass; the Statue of Liberty–Ellis Island Foundation and an NPS museum page say approximately **250** amber-coloured cathedral-glass panels/plates. Both are credible sources. Safest on air: **"hundreds of panes of amber cathedral glass."** — https://www.npshistory.com/handbooks/historical/11/hh11k.htm ; https://www.statueofliberty.org/statue-of-liberty/overview-history/ ; https://nps.gov/elis/learn/historyculture/history-of-the-statue-of-liberty.htm
185. NPS's own teaching text puts it bluntly: **"the last modification was replacing almost all the copper with stained glass to allow more light."** — NPS, "Statue of Liberty Pedestal and Museum Self-Guided Tour" (PDF) https://www.nps.gov/common/uploads/teachers/lessonplans/PedestalMuseum%20Self-Guided%20Tour.pdf
186. **And that is what killed it.** "Leaks from rain and corrosion from the elements had damaged the original torch above the handle beyond repair." A flame full of glass panes is a flame full of gaps. — Statue of Liberty–Ellis Island Foundation, "Overview + History" https://www.statueofliberty.org/statue-of-liberty/overview-history/
187. **The original torch was removed on 4 July 1984** — the oldest part of the statue, taken down after 98 years. — Statue of Liberty–Ellis Island Foundation, "Overview + History" https://www.statueofliberty.org/statue-of-liberty/overview-history/ ; NPS, "Frequently Asked Questions" https://www.nps.gov/stli/faqs.htm
188. **The original torch weighs 3,600 pounds.** — NPS, "Statue of Liberty's Original Torch Completes Move to New Home" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/news/statue-of-liberty-s-original-torch-completes-move-to-new-home.htm
189. **In November 1985 a replica of Bartholdi's original design was installed** — solid copper again, as he intended — and **formally dedicated during the Liberty Weekend centennial on 4 July 1986.** — Statue of Liberty–Ellis Island Foundation, "Overview + History" https://www.statueofliberty.org/statue-of-liberty/overview-history/
190. **The new flame is covered in 24-karat gold leaf**, gilded according to Bartholdi's original plans. It is not lit from within — **it is lit from outside and shines by reflection**, which is what Bartholdi wanted all along. — NPS, "Frequently Asked Questions" https://www.nps.gov/stli/faqs.htm ; NPS, "Restoring the Statue" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/places_restoring.htm
191. **On 15 November 2018 the original torch was moved 470 feet across the island** to the new Statue of Liberty Museum, on a hydraulically stabilised transporter and then by crane. It is in the Inspiration Gallery. — NPS, "Statue of Liberty's Original Torch Completes Move to New Home" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/news/statue-of-liberty-s-original-torch-completes-move-to-new-home.htm
192. **The torch has never been open to the public** in its current configuration; it is reached by **a narrow 40-foot ladder.** — NPS, "Frequently Asked Questions" https://www.nps.gov/stli/faqs.htm

### She used to be brown
193. **She was not green. She was the colour of a new penny** — NPS describes the original as bronze, brown or dark grey. — NPS, "Preserving a Symbol" https://www.nps.gov/teachers/classrooms/preserving-a-symbol.htm
194. **"The National Park Service believes that the oxidation process took about 25 to 30 years to fully change from bronze to green."** — NPS, "Preserving a Symbol" https://www.nps.gov/teachers/classrooms/preserving-a-symbol.htm
195. **NPS's FAQ gives roughly 30 years for full oxidation.** — NPS, "Frequently Asked Questions" https://www.nps.gov/stli/faqs.htm
196. **The consequence for section 5, and it is a strong one:** the peak Ellis Island generation sailed past a statue that was **changing colour in front of them** — brown in the 1880s, blotchy through the 1890s, fully green by roughly the 1910s. **No two arriving families saw quite the same statue.**
197. The patina is not damage — it is **a protective film on the copper** formed by oxidation. — NPS, "Preserving a Symbol" https://www.nps.gov/teachers/classrooms/preserving-a-symbol.htm
198. **She is struck by lightning many times each year**, being tall and copper. — NPS, "Frequently Asked Questions" https://www.nps.gov/stli/faqs.htm

### Black Tom, 1916
199. **In the early hours of 30 July 1916, a munitions depot on Black Tom Island, Jersey City, was deliberately sabotaged** to stop supplies reaching Britain and France in the First World War. — NPS, "Domestic Sabotage: The Explosion at Black Tom Island" https://www.nps.gov/articles/black-tom-wwi.htm
200. **The first explosion came shortly before 2 a.m.; the largest at 2:05 a.m.** The munitions were held in warehouses, railroad box cars and on barges belonging to the **Lehigh Valley Railroad**. — NPS, "Domestic Sabotage" https://www.nps.gov/articles/black-tom-wwi.htm
201. **Shrapnel embedded itself in the right side of the Statue of Liberty.** — NPS, "Domestic Sabotage" https://www.nps.gov/articles/black-tom-wwi.htm
202. **The shock wave pushed the torch arm against the crown of the statue, damaging the internal framework.** — NPS, "Domestic Sabotage" https://www.nps.gov/articles/black-tom-wwi.htm
203. **The torch stayed lit through the whole thing.** — NPS, "Domestic Sabotage" https://www.nps.gov/articles/black-tom-wwi.htm
204. **The torch has been closed to the public ever since — since 1916.** NPS calls Black Tom **"one of the largest acts of sabotage to our nation prior to the event of Pearl Harbor."** — NPS, "Frequently Asked Questions About the Statue of Liberty" https://www.nps.gov/stli/planyourvisit/frequently-asked-questions-statue-of-liberty.htm
205. **The sharpest way to say it:** a German sabotage operation in New Jersey in 1916 is the reason no visitor has stood in that torch for over a century. The closure is not about safety rules — it is a war wound.
206. **Timing detail worth noting:** 1916 is both the year she was damaged by Black Tom (July) and the year her lighting was finally fixed and the torch reglazed by Borglum. Same year, same torch, two opposite stories.

### Other surprises
207. **She is the reason the island has its name, not the other way round.** Bedloe's Island was **renamed Liberty Island in 1956** by joint resolution of Congress, signed by President Eisenhower — **70 years after the statue arrived.** — NPS, "Liberty Island Chronology" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/liberty-island-a-chronology.htm
208. **She was not a national monument until 1924** — **38 years after her dedication.** On **15 October 1924** a presidential proclamation by **Calvin Coolidge** declared Fort Wood, and the statue within it, a National Monument. — NPS, "History Continued" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/history-continued.htm ; NPS, "Liberty Island Chronology" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/liberty-island-a-chronology.htm
209. **Note the boundary oddity:** the 1924 monument boundary was drawn at **Fort Wood's outer edge** — the monument was legally the fort, with the statue inside it. Jurisdiction expanded to the whole island only on **7 September 1937.** — NPS, "History Continued" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/history-continued.htm
210. The National Park Service took over administration in **1933**. — NPS, "History Continued" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/history-continued.htm
211. **Ellis Island was added to the monument on 11 May 1965** by presidential proclamation of **Lyndon B. Johnson** — which is why one ferry ticket covers both. — NPS, "Liberty Island Chronology" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/liberty-island-a-chronology.htm
212. **The 1980s restoration, 1982–1986:** President Reagan appointed **Lee Iacocca** in May 1982 to head the effort; it was a public-private partnership costing **$86 million**; the restored statue reopened **5 July 1986.** — NPS, "History Continued" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/history-continued.htm
213. **What they found:** holes in the copper skin, and **rusting iron armature bars** connecting the skin to the skeleton. Eiffel's iron was destroying her from the inside. — NPS, "Restoring the Statue" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/places_restoring.htm
214. **The fix: every original iron armature bar was replaced with stainless steel.** About **1,000 workers** from many trades worked on it. — NPS, "Restoring the Statue" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/historyculture/places_restoring.htm ; NPS, "History of the Statue of Liberty" https://nps.gov/elis/learn/historyculture/history-of-the-statue-of-liberty.htm
215. **She is a UNESCO World Heritage Site**, recognised in part for **"the use of electricity to light the torch"** and the **flexible curtain-wall construction** — her engineering, not just her meaning. — NPS, "Statue of Liberty: World Heritage Site" https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/statue-of-liberty-national-monument-world-heritage-site.htm
216. **Roughly 3.8 million people visit each year.** — NPS, "Statue of Liberty Facts" https://www.nps.gov/stli/learn/statue-of-liberty-facts.htm

**The answer: 162 steps from the top of the pedestal to the crown, and 377 steps from the ground to the crown.**

217. **"162 stairs are narrow and steep in dimension"** from the top of the pedestal to the crown. — NPS, "Physical Mobility in the Crown" https://www.nps.gov/stli/planyourvisit/physical-mobility-in-the-crown.htm
218. **215 steps from the ground to the top of the pedestal:** "There are 215 steps or approximately 10 stories to climb to the top of the pedestal." — NPS, "Visit the Pedestal" https://www.nps.gov/stli/planyourvisit/visiting-the-pedestal.htm
219. **215 + 162 = 377 steps, ground to crown.** NPS states the crown is **162 steps above the top of the pedestal and 377 steps above the ground.** — NPS, "Physical Mobility in the Crown" https://www.nps.gov/stli/planyourvisit/physical-mobility-in-the-crown.htm
220. **Where the 146 comes from — and it is not an error.** The 162-step crown climb breaks into **an initial 16 steps on a 22-inch-wide standard staircase, then "146 steps remaining to the Crown Platform"** on the narrow spiral. So **146 is a genuine NPS number — it is the spiral portion only**, not the whole climb. — NPS, "Physical Mobility in the Crown" https://www.nps.gov/stli/planyourvisit/physical-mobility-in-the-crown.htm
221. **Where the 354 comes from:** 354 is the figure in wide popular circulation for the pedestal-to-crown or ground-to-crown climb. **It does not match any current NPS figure** (NPS gives 162, 215 and 377). **[UNVERIFIED]** — no NPS page located in this research states 354. **Do not use 354.**
222. **Stair dimensions, if you want the physical detail:** each step is **9 inches high and 8 inches long**; there are **44 continuous steps before the first of four rest platforms**; head clearance on the spiral is low, and NPS warns visitors six feet or taller to watch their heads. — NPS, "Physical Mobility in the Crown" https://www.nps.gov/stli/planyourvisit/physical-mobility-in-the-crown.htm
223. **Children must be 42 inches (1.1 m) or taller** to climb to the crown — relevant for a family in the queue. — NPS, "Physical Mobility in the Crown" https://www.nps.gov/stli/planyourvisit/physical-mobility-in-the-crown.htm
224. **There is an elevator to the top of the pedestal** for those who cannot use the stairs; there is **no elevator to the crown.** — NPS, "Visit the Pedestal" https://www.nps.gov/stli/planyourvisit/visiting-the-pedestal.htm
225. **Safe narration:** "Three hundred and seventy-seven steps from the ground to her crown — and the last hundred and sixty-two of them up a spiral staircase eight inches deep."

### Other contested numbers (full detail at the line numbers given)
226. **Castle Clinton build start:** 1807 vs 1808 — see fact 3. Every NPS source agrees on **completed 1811**; a salute was fired 25 November 1811.
226a. **Castle Clinton's designer:** popularly "designed by Jonathan Williams"; NPS's own Historic Structures Report says **the evidence is not conclusive** — see fact 9c. Say "built under," not "designed by."
226b. **Castle Garden opening:** 1 August 1855 (formal opening) vs 3 August 1855 (first arrivals) — **not a conflict**, see fact 18a.
226c. **Morse's telegraph here:** WPA Guide says 1835, NPS says **1842** — the WPA Guide is wrong, see fact 16t.
226d. **Castle Garden's total arrivals:** 8,280,917 (NPS/Svejda) vs 7,690,606 (WPA Guide 1939) vs "some 8,000,000" (Senate Report 1946) vs ~7,500,000 (LPC 1965) — **use 8,280,917**, the only figure with a stated span and denominator. See facts 21 and 38h.
226e. **Jenny Lind's charity gift:** $10,000 (Barnum) vs $12,600 (NYC Parks) — see fact 16q. Say "every dollar of her share."
226f. **Aquarium attendance:** 84,336,316 (NPS, 39 yrs) vs "more than seventy-six million" by end-1938 (WPA Guide) — **consistent, not a conflict.** See fact 38m.
226g. **The 1941 threat:** one NPS page says a **bridge** stopped by WWII; the National Register nomination and LPC say the **tunnel**, stopped by citizens. **The tunnel and the citizens** — the bridge died in July 1939. The NPS "places" page is the outlier.
226h. **1950 transfer day and 1975 reopening day:** both circulate (18 July 1950; 24 May 1975) but **no government source gives either day.** Use the years. See facts 38ak and 38ao.
226i. **Annie Moore's age:** 17 (NPS) vs "a teenager" (NPS) vs 13 (NARA) — see fact 197a. Say "a teenager from County Cork."
226j. **Ellis Island fire:** 14 vs 15 June 1897, both NPS — see fact 200a. Say "mid-June 1897."
226k. **Ellis Island rejection rate:** NPS says **one percent**, not the commonly cited two — see fact 216a.
227. **Castle Clinton renaming:** 1815 vs 1817 — see fact 11.
228. **Castle Garden closing date:** 18 April vs 30 April 1890 — see fact 30.
229. **Castle Garden's share of arrivals:** two-thirds vs three-quarters — see fact 22.
230. **Copper and total weight:** three different NPS figures — see fact 114.
231. **Length of nose:** 4 ft 6 in vs 4 ft 0 in — see fact 113.
232. **Pedestal height:** 87 ft design vs 89 ft as built — see fact 121.
233. **Torch glass panes:** 600 vs ~250 — see fact 184.
234. **Statue pieces:** "350 pieces in 214 crates" is consistent across NPS and the Foundation. One NPS page loosely says the statue "was disassembled into 350 pieces" without mentioning crates; these are the same fact, not a conflict. — https://www.statueofliberty.org/statue-of-liberty/overview-history/

### Added from the archival lanes — the strongest speakable lines
245. **Stephen Graham, 1914 (public domain) — the single best quotation in this fact sheet:** "An American waved a starry flag in greeting, and some emigrants were disposed to cheer, some shed silent tears. 248. **Steiner, 1906 — the silence before it:** "The steerage is still mute; it looks to the left at the populous shore, to the right at the green stretches of Long Island, and again straight ahead at the mighty city." — *On the Trail of the Immigrant*, 1906

254. **Congress, 1946, on this building:** "**Through its gates passed some 8,000,000 future Americans** whose grandchildren and great grandchildren have in our day fought to preserve our common national heritage." — S. Rept. 79-1899, 29 July 1946
