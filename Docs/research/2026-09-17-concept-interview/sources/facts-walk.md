## STOP 1a — FEDERAL HALL, 26 WALL ST: ARCHITECTURE

1. The present building was begun in May 1834 and finished in May 1841, opening as the U.S. Custom House in 1842. — HABS NY-470 (1935 report), n.d./1935; NPS-FEHA https://www.nps.gov/feha/learn/hc.htm
2. A design competition was held in 1833; first prize went to Ithiel Town and Alexander Jackson Davis. — HABS NY-470, 1935
3. The commissioners disliked Town & Davis's interior and engaged William Ross, an English architect then in New York, to redesign it; Ross's plan is substantially what exists. — HABS NY-470, 1935
4. John Frazee superintended almost the entire construction and "is generally known as the architect," preparing most working drawings while keeping Town & Davis's exterior and Ross's interior plan. — HABS NY-470, 1935
5. The National Register form credits the building's Greek Revival design to John Frazee, with a significant year of 1834. — NRHP-66000095, 1972
6. The exterior is Greek Doric with hexastyle (six-column) porticoes on the Wall Street (south) and Pine Street (north) fronts. — HABS NY-470, 1935. NOTE: an 1840 newspaper account quoted in the same HABS file, and the NRHP form, both say **eight** columns per portico; see Contested Claims.
7. Because Pine Street sits much higher than Wall Street, the south front needs eighteen granite steps the full width of the building while the north front has only three. — HABS NY-470, 1935
8. The nomination describes the eight Doric columns at each end and the low triangular pediment as recalling "the Parthenon, the temple of the goddess Athena on the Acropolis in Athens." — NRHP-66000095, 1972
9. A period account (James Miller, *New York As It Is*, 1863) quoted in the HABS file says it is built of white marble "something after the model of the Parthenon at Athens." — HABS NY-470, quoting Miller, 1863
10. The marble was dolomite from a quarry near Tuckahoe, Westchester County, New York — per John Frazee's granddaughter, who held his drawings. — HABS NY-470, 1935
11. The building was made deliberately fireproof: marble, limestone, granite, brick and iron, with no structural wood. — NRHP-66000095, 1972; HABS NY-470, 1935
12. The roof is built of overlapping marble slabs carried on brick arches; the slabs lap eight inches with an upper and under lip to allow for expansion and exclude water. — HABS NY-470, 1935 (quoting an 1841 account)
13. Interior floors are groin-vaulted masonry throughout; doors, sashes and railings were iron, originally painted white. — HABS NY-470, quoting an 1840 *Evening Post* piece reprinted in *The New World*
14. The rotunda sits at the south end of a vaulted entrance hall and rises through the second-floor level, lit by a lantern piercing the crown of an elliptical domed ceiling. — HABS NY-470, 1935
15. Period accounts give the rotunda as about 60 feet clear diameter, ringed by 16 polished marble Corinthian columns roughly 30 ft high and 2 ft 8 in in diameter, each a solid piece. — HABS NY-470, quoting Barber & Howe, *Historical Collections of the State of New York*, 1841, and the 1840 *Evening Post* account
16. The Corinthian capitals were copied from those of the Temple of Jupiter Stator in Rome. — HABS NY-470, quoting the 1840 *Evening Post* account
17. Cast-iron galleries ring the rotunda at second-floor level, their railings formed of caryatid figures holding acanthus scrolls. — HABS NY-470, 1935
18. The building measures roughly 200 ft long by 90 ft wide and about 60 ft high from foundation to top. — HABS NY-470, quoting Barber & Howe, 1841
19. The largest marble blocks used weigh thirty-three tons. — HABS NY-470, quoting Barber & Howe, 1841
20. Cost: the 1841 account gives $1,175,000 including ground, $950,000 for the building alone; a separate HABS sheet gives $939,301. — HABS NY-470 (both figures appear in the same file)
21. Two self-supporting spiral stone stairs run from the rotunda to the third floor; the main stair is made of one-piece interlocking granite blocks cantilevered from the wall. — HABS NY-470, 1935
22. Greek Revival was popular in America c. 1830s–1860s because "many Americans viewed their country as the natural heirs to the ancient Greeks, who invented democracy"; banks, offices, churches and houses were all turned into small Greek temples. — NPS, "Greek Revival Style 1830s–1860s," n.d. https://www.nps.gov/articles/greek-revival-architecture.htm
23. The Custom House became the U.S. Sub-Treasury in 1862 — the most important of six, holding some 70 percent of federal revenues; the Independent Treasury System was discarded after 1920. — NRHP-66000095, 1972 (NPS-FEHA gives 1862 for the Sub-Treasury and 1920 for the Federal Reserve taking over)
24. Designated a National Historic Site in 1939 and established as a National Memorial in 1955. — NRHP-66000095, 1972
25. The building the present one replaced — the colonial City Hall / Federal Hall — stood on this site from 1699 and was demolished in 1812 once the current City Hall was finished. — NRHP-66000095, 1972; WPA 1939
26. The WPA Guide's own 1939 description: a "dignified structure designed in Greek-Revival style by Ithiel Town and A. J. Davis," then housing the New York Passport Agency. — WPA 1939, p. ~87

## STOP 1b — FEDERAL HALL: REVOLUTION AND THE EARLY REPUBLIC

27. The earlier building on this site served as New York's second City Hall; NPS dates its construction to between 1699 and 1703. — NPS-FEHA https://www.nps.gov/feha/learn/hc.htm (the 1972 NRHP form says "completion in 1699"; WPA 1939 says "built in 1699")
28. John Peter Zenger, printer of the *New-York Weekly Journal*, was jailed and tried here in 1735 for seditious libel against Governor William Cosby. — NPS, "The Trial of John Peter Zenger," n.d. https://www.nps.gov/feha/learn/historyculture/the-trial-of-john-peter-zenger.htm ; NRHP-66000095, 1972
29. Zenger first appeared in court 16 April 1735; the trial itself opened 4 August 1735. — NPS, "The Trial of John Peter Zenger," n.d.
30. Chief Justice James DeLancey disbarred Zenger's original attorneys, James Alexander and William Smith, and assigned John Chambers; Andrew Hamilton of Philadelphia then took the defense. — NPS, "The Trial of John Peter Zenger," n.d.
31. DeLancey from the bench: "The laws in my opinion are very clear; they cannot be admitted to justify a libel." — NPS, "The Trial of John Peter Zenger," n.d. (quoting the trial record)
32. Hamilton's defense was that truth is a defense against a charge of libel; the jury, foreman Thomas Hunt, returned Not Guilty. — NPS, "The Trial of John Peter Zenger," n.d.
33. The WPA Guide calls the Zenger verdict the outcome of "the country's first major battle for freedom of the press." — WPA 1939
34. The Stamp Act Congress met in this building in October 1765. — NRHP-66000095, 1972; WPA 1939
35. Delegates from nine of the thirteen colonies attended; the Declaration of Rights and Grievances is dated 19 October 1765. — U.S. House of Representatives History, Art & Archives, "Declaration of Rights and Grievances," n.d. https://history.house.gov/Records-and-Research/Listing/c_102/ ; Library of Congress, "No Taxation Without Representation," *Magna Carta: Muse and Mentor*, n.d. https://loc.gov/exhibits/magna-carta-muse-and-mentor/no-taxation-without-representation.html
36. Its third point: "it is inseparably essential to the freedom of the people, and the undoubted right of Englishmen, that no taxes should be imposed on them but with their own consent, given personally or by their representatives." — Library of Congress, *Magna Carta: Muse and Mentor*, n.d.
37. The Confederation Congress sat here after the war and passed the Northwest Ordinance in 1787, in the same year calling for the Constitutional Convention in Philadelphia. — NRHP-66000095, 1972
38. After the Constitution was ratified in 1788, Pierre Charles L'Enfant — later planner of Washington, D.C. — remodelled the building as the nation's capitol. — NRHP-66000095, 1972; NPS-FEHA; WPA 1939
39. George Washington took the oath of office as first President on 30 April 1789, on the second-floor balcony, sworn in by Chancellor Robert R. Livingston, before crowds in the street below. — NPS, "Inaugural Balcony," n.d. https://home.nps.gov/feha/learn/historyculture/inaugural-balcony.htm
40. He wore an American-made dark brown suit with white silk stockings and silver shoe buckles. — NPS, "Inaugural Balcony," n.d.
41. He then delivered the inaugural address to a joint session in the Senate Chamber inside the building. — National Archives, "George Washington's First Inaugural Address," n.d. https://www.archives.gov/legislative/features/gw-inauguration ; Library of Congress, "Forging a Federal Government," n.d.
42. The delivered address ran eight manuscript pages, replacing an earlier draft of more than seventy. — Library of Congress, "Forging a Federal Government," *Creating the United States*, n.d. https://loc.gov/exhibits/creating-the-united-states/forging-a-federal-government.html
43. One observer noted Washington spoke in a deep, low voice betraying "manifest embarrassment." — Library of Congress, "Today in History — April 30," n.d. https://www.loc.gov/item/today-in-history/april-30
44. The inauguration was originally set for 4 March 1789 but slipped because members of the new Congress arrived late and the electoral ballots could not be counted on time. — Library of Congress, "Today in History — April 30," n.d.
45. A large brownstone slab of the original balcony floor survived the 1812 demolition, was displayed for years at Bellevue Hospital, and was returned to the site in 1889 for the inauguration centennial. — NPS, "Inaugural Balcony," n.d.
46. That stone is now displayed in the lower rotunda; a crack from installation exposes the original stone under a cement skim coat added to carry an inscription. — NPS, "Inaugural Balcony," n.d.
47. The First Congress met here and created the Departments of State, Treasury, War and Justice, the Post Office, and — via the Judiciary Act — the Supreme Court and lower federal courts, all in 1789. — NRHP-66000095, 1972
48. Congress passed twelve proposed constitutional amendments by joint resolution on 25 September 1789; Washington signed the transmittal on 2 October 1789 and sent copies to the eleven ratifying states. — U.S. Senate, "Congress Submits the First Constitutional Amendments to the States," n.d. https://www.senate.gov/about/origins-foundations/senate-and-constitution/congress-submits-first-amendments-to-states.htm ; National Archives, "Bill of Rights," n.d. https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/bill-of-rights
49. Ten of those twelve were ratified 15 December 1791 and became the Bill of Rights; Article 2 was finally ratified in 1992 as the 27th Amendment; Article 1 never was. — National Archives, "Bill of Rights (1791)," n.d.
50. The capital moved to Philadelphia in August 1790 and the building reverted to use as City Hall. — NRHP-66000095, 1972
51. J.Q.A. Ward's bronze statue of Washington on the Wall Street steps was erected in 1883; the WPA Guide carefully says it marks "the place above the steps where it is claimed he stood." — WPA 1939 (see Contested Claims re. an OCR date error in the NRHP form)
52. The wide bank of Wall Street steps "has always been a favorite place for public rallies, political and religious oratory, war-bond sales, and patriotic observances." — NRHP-66000095, 1972
53. British occupation context for the site: British forces landed at Kip's Bay and took the city on 15 September 1776 and held New York until 1783. — Trinity Church Wall Street, "1776: Trinity Church and the American Revolution," n.d. https://trinitychurchnyc.org/stories-news/1776-trinity-church-and-american-revolution ; WPA 1939 notes the British "occupied Manhattan for almost the entire duration of the war"

## STOP 2 — TRINITY CHURCH, BROADWAY AT WALL ST

54. The parish charter was signed 6 May 1697 by Governor Benjamin Fletcher, in the reign of King William III. — WPA 1939; NHL-76001252, c.1976; Trinity Church, "Trinity Church: A Historic House of Worship," n.d. https://trinitychurchnyc.org/visit/trinity-church
55. The first church opened in 1698, facing west toward the Hudson. — WPA 1939; Trinity Church, n.d.
56. In 1705 Queen Anne granted the parish 215 acres running from Fulton Street north to Christopher Street, and from Greenwich Street east to Broadway. — Trinity Church, "A Historic House of Worship," n.d.
57. The WPA Guide quotes the grant as land "for the benefit of said Church and other pious uses," at a yearly rent of thirty pounds, "a reasonable request." — WPA 1939
58. The Great Fire began around midnight on 20–21 September 1776 near Whitehall Slip and burned roughly a quarter of the city in about twelve hours. — Trinity Church, "1776: Trinity Church and the American Revolution," n.d.
59. The first Trinity Church was destroyed in that fire, along with Trinity's £850 organ, the Rector's House and the Charity School; 200 houses on church ground burned; damage to Trinity property was put at £25,000. — Trinity Church, "1776," n.d.
60. St. Paul's Chapel survived, largely because Rev. Charles Inglis organised firefighting. — Trinity Church, "1776," n.d.
61. The cause of the 1776 fire was disputed at the time and was never settled. — Trinity Church, "1776," n.d.
62. A contemporary newspaper described the burning steeple — "which was 140 feet high, the upper Part of Wood" — as resembling "a vast Pyramid of Fire." — quoted in Trinity Church, "1776," n.d. (period newspaper, 1776; public domain)
63. The second church opened in 1790, with a steeple of about 200 feet; George Washington, then President, attended. — Trinity Church, "A Historic House of Worship," n.d. (WPA 1939 dates the rebuilding from 1787)
64. In 1839 heavy snow collapsed the second church's roof supports; Richard Upjohn was brought in to repair it and instead rebuilt entirely. — Trinity Church, "10 Fast Facts About Trinity Church," n.d. https://trinitychurchnyc.org/stories-news/top-ten-things-know-about-trinity-church-wall-street
65. Upjohn delivered drawings and plans on 9 September 1839, proposing a spire fifty feet higher than its predecessor; the church was estimated at $85,000 in stone with a wooden spire, $5,000 more for a stone spire — the Corporation chose stone. — NHL-76001252, quoting John Coolidge, Harvard honors thesis, 1935
66. The cornerstone was laid 3 June 1841 in "the North East buttress of the tower"; the building committee record names Richard Upjohn architect, James Thorn sculptor, James Vandenbergh master mason, Samuel Martin master carpenter. — NHL-76001252, c.1976
67. The third church was consecrated on Ascension Day 1846. — Trinity Church, "A Historic House of Worship," n.d.; NHL-76001252 gives completion 1846
68. It is built of dark brownstone in a free rendering of perpendicular English Gothic, only 79 feet wide and 166 feet long. — WPA 1939; NHL-76001252
69. Upjohn's design is based on a drawing by A.W. Pugin in *True Principles*, with the deep chancel omitted and the roof pitch modified. — NHL-76001252, c.1976
70. Upjohn was an ardent High Churchman and fitted the chancel with choir stalls, but anti-papist feeling was strong enough that they were not used at first. — NHL-76001252, quoting Coolidge, 1935
71. The square tower is double-buttressed at each corner by buttresses that diminish in depth as they rise; four ornate pinnacles with flying buttresses behind them make the transition to the octagonal spire. — NHL-76001252, quoting NYC Landmarks Commission
72. Spire height: Trinity's own history gives 281 feet; the WPA Guide gives 280 feet "above the steps." — Trinity Church, n.d.; WPA 1939. Use "roughly 280 feet" if you want to stay safe.
73. Trinity claims to have been the tallest building in the United States for 23 years (1846 until St. Michael's Church, Chicago, in 1869) and the tallest in New York City until 1890, when the New York World Building (348 ft) passed it. — Trinity Church, "The Tallest Building in New York City," n.d. https://trinitychurchnyc.org/stories-news/tallest-building-new-york-city
74. The WPA Guide, more modestly: "For years, the spire... served as a landmark." — WPA 1939
75. Tower and spire are brownstone ashlar, "exceptionally fine in workmanship." — WPA 1939
76. The first "Ring of Bells," a gift from London, arrived in 1797 and is the oldest in the city; the chime numbered ten bells by 1939. — WPA 1939
77. Three pairs of bronze doors by Richard Morris Hunt were the gift of William Waldorf Astor as a memorial to his father, designed after Ghiberti's Baptistery doors in Florence; panels by Karl Bitter, J. Massey Rhind and C. H. Niehaus. — WPA 1939
78. Alexander Hamilton fought his duel with Aaron Burr on the morning of 11 July 1804 at Weehawken, died the afternoon of 12 July, and was buried from Trinity on 14 July 1804. — Trinity Church, "An Anniversary Week: Recalling Alexander Hamilton's Duel, Death & Funeral," n.d. https://trinitychurchnyc.org/stories-news/anniversary-week-recalling-alexander-hamiltons-duel-death-funeral
79. Hamilton lies near the southern fence of the churchyard; his monument stands above the simpler vault stone of his wife. — Trinity Church, "Alexander Hamilton and Trinity Church," n.d. https://trinitychurchnyc.org/visit-history/historical-highlights/alexander-hamilton
80. Gouverneur Morris delivered the funeral address from a stage at Trinity's portico, with Hamilton's four sons present, aged roughly six to sixteen. — Trinity Church, "An Anniversary Week," n.d.
81. Elizabeth Schuyler Hamilton (1757–1854) outlived him by fifty years, dying at 97, and is buried beside him. — Trinity Church, "Elizabeth Schuyler Hamilton: An Influential Trailblazer," n.d. https://trinitychurchnyc.org/stories-news/elizabeth-schuyler-hamilton-influential-trailblazer
82. Trinity records show Eliza on an 1801 communion list; the parish notes its records do NOT confirm that Alexander himself worshipped there regularly. — Trinity Church, "10 Fast Facts," n.d.
83. The churchyard also holds Robert Fulton, Captain James Lawrence, Albert Gallatin, William Bradford, John Watts, and Francis Lewis — the only signer of the Declaration buried in Manhattan. — NHL-76001252, quoting NYC Landmarks Commission; WPA 1939
84. Captain James Lawrence was mortally wounded 1 June 1813 commanding USS *Chesapeake* against HMS *Shannon* and died 4 June en route to Halifax as a prize. — Naval History and Heritage Command, "Lawrence, James," n.d. https://www.history.navy.mil/our-collections/photography/us-people/l/lawrence-james.html
85. He was first buried at Halifax, then reinterred in Trinity Churchyard on 16 September 1813 before a large public service. — Naval History and Heritage Command, n.d.
86. The *Chesapeake–Shannon* action was the bloodiest single-ship fight of the war, with more than 280 killed or wounded. — Naval History and Heritage Command, n.d.
87. The churchyard served as a public burial ground from the 1660s; the oldest legible stone is Richard Churcher, died 1681 — sixteen years before Trinity was a congregation. — Trinity Church, "10 Fast Facts," n.d.
88. The Martyrs' Monument, to American patriots who died in British prisons in New York, stands near the Broadway–Thames Street corner. — WPA 1939
89. Washington worshipped at Trinity and at St. Paul's; after the 1789 inauguration "he and members of both houses of Congress walked six blocks north from Wall Street to attend a service at St. Paul's Chapel." — Trinity Church, "10 Fast Facts," n.d.
90. The building is one of the earliest and finest American Gothic Revival works and the commission that made Upjohn's national reputation. — NHL-76001252, c.1976

## STOP 3 — 9/11 MEMORIAL PLAZA (plaza only)

Handle with restraint; every figure below is the institution's own.
Primary source: 9/11 Memorial & Museum, "About the Memorial," n.d. https://www.911memorial.org/visit/memorial/about-memorial and the "9/11 Memorial & Museum Institutional Backgrounder," n.d. https://www.911memorial.org/sites/default/files/inline-files/911%20Memorial%20&%20Museum%20Institutional%20Backgrounder.pdf

91. The design, "Reflecting Absence," is by architect Michael Arad with landscape architect Peter Walker; it was selected as the competition winner in January 2004. — 9/11 Memorial, "About the Memorial," n.d.
92. The Memorial occupies eight of the 16 acres of the World Trade Center site. — Institutional Backgrounder, n.d.
93. Two reflecting pools sit in the footprints of the North and South Towers, each nearly an acre in size. — "About the Memorial," n.d.
94. Each pool side is approximately 200 feet long. — Institutional Backgrounder, n.d.
95. The waterfalls drop 30 feet into each square basin — the largest man-made waterfalls in North America. — "About the Memorial," n.d.; Institutional Backgrounder, n.d.
96. From the basin the water falls a further 20 feet and disappears into a smaller central void. — "About the Memorial," n.d.
97. The plaza holds 413 swamp white oaks. — Institutional Backgrounder, n.d. (the commonly quoted "about 400" is a rounding of this)
98. The oaks were drawn from areas native to all three 9/11 crash sites. — "About the Memorial," n.d.
99. One Callery pear, the "Survivor Tree," is original to the site. — Institutional Backgrounder, n.d.
100. It was found at Ground Zero in October 2001, severely damaged, "with snapped roots and burned and broken branches." — Institutional Backgrounder, n.d.
101. It was removed from the rubble and placed in the care of the NYC Department of Parks and Recreation, and returned to the Memorial in 2010. — Institutional Backgrounder, n.d.
102. "New, smooth limbs extend from the gnarled stumps, creating a visible demarcation between the tree's past and present." — Institutional Backgrounder, n.d. (quotable verbatim; institutional, not public domain)
103. 2,983 names are inscribed on the bronze parapets: the 2,977 killed at the three sites on 11 September 2001 and the six killed in the 26 February 1993 WTC bombing. — Institutional Backgrounder, n.d.
104. Of those killed on 9/11: 2,753 in New York, 184 at the Pentagon, 40 aboard Flight 93 near Shanksville, Pennsylvania. — Institutional Backgrounder, n.d.
105. 441 first responders from more than 30 agencies died on 9/11, including 343 FDNY firefighters, 37 Port Authority Police officers and 23 NYPD officers. — Institutional Backgrounder, n.d.
106. Names are grouped by location and circumstance, then arranged within those groups by "meaningful adjacencies." — "About the Memorial," n.d.
107. Next of kin were invited to request that a name be inscribed beside specific others; more than 1,200 such requests were honored. — Institutional Backgrounder, n.d.; "About the Memorial," n.d.
108. Some requests were for relatives, friends and colleagues; others were for people the victim "may have barely known or just met, but with whom intense bonds were quickly formed as a result of shared response." — Institutional Backgrounder, n.d.
109. Names also keep together the crews of each of the four flights and the first-responder agencies and units. — "About the Memorial," n.d.
110. Every name has a panel address: N or S for North or South Pool, plus a number from 1 to 76. — Institutional Backgrounder, n.d.
111. The names arrangement followed a multi-year verification process before the 2011 opening; all inscribed names are of people who died as a direct result of injury sustained in the attacks. — Institutional Backgrounder, n.d.
112. The Memorial was dedicated on the tenth anniversary in a ceremony for victims' families and opened to the public on 12 September 2011. — Institutional Backgrounder, n.d.
113. The 9/11 Memorial Glade, on the southwest corner of the plaza, was dedicated 30 May 2019 — the 18th anniversary of the formal end of rescue and recovery at Ground Zero. — Institutional Backgrounder, n.d.
114. The Glade, also by Arad and Walker, is a pathway flanked by six stone monoliths, honoring those sick or dead from exposure to toxins in the aftermath. — Institutional Backgrounder, n.d.
115. The monoliths are inlaid with World Trade Center steel. — 9/11 Memorial, "About the Memorial," n.d.
116. The museum pavilion between the two pools is by the Norwegian firm Snøhetta; two steel tridents from the Twin Towers' facade stand in its glass atrium. — Institutional Backgrounder, n.d. (mention only if you refer to the building you walk past)
117. 11.6 million people visited the Memorial plaza in 2025. — Institutional Backgrounder, n.d.

## STOP 4 — BOWLING GREEN

118. NYC Parks: "Bowling Green Park, the first official park in New York, was established and named by a resolution of the Common Council on March 12, 1733." — NYC Parks, "The Oldest Parks," n.d. https://www.nycgovparks.org/about/history/timeline/oldest-parks
119. It was leased at an annual rent of one peppercorn to John Chambers, Peter Bayard and Peter Jay, who were to improve it with grass, trees and a wooden fence. — NYC Parks, "The Oldest Parks," n.d.
120. The lease's stated purpose, verbatim: "for the Beauty and Ornament of the Said Street as well as for the Recreation & Delight of the Inhabitants of this City." — NYC Parks, "The Oldest Parks," n.d. (quoting the 1733 Common Council record)
121. The WPA Guide dates the same lease to 1732 and calls the park "probably the city's oldest public park" — note both the year and the hedge. — WPA 1939
122. NPS calls Bowling Green "New York City's oldest public park" and traces municipal ownership to the 1686 Dongan Charter, which put "waste, vacant, un-patented and unappropriated lands" in city hands. — NPS, "Bowling Green," n.d. https://www.nps.gov/places/bowling-green.htm ; NYC Parks, "The Oldest Parks," n.d.
123. Before that, the plot was part of the Marcktveldt hog and cattle market (1638–47) and later a Dutch militia parade ground. — WPA 1939
124. The gilded lead equestrian statue of George III was commissioned by New York merchants grateful for the repeal of the Stamp Act and was the first statue in a city park. — NYC Parks, "The Oldest Parks," n.d.
125. It was unveiled 16 August 1770. — LPC LP-0548, 1970
126. The iron fence was erected in 1771 to protect the park and the statue, with the stated purpose of insuring the Green not become "a Recipticle (sic) of all the filth and dirt of the neighborhood." — LPC LP-0548, 1970
127. The original construction contract, with Richard Sharpe, Peter T. Curtenius, Gilbert Forbes and Andrew Lyall, called for an iron fence set in a stone foundation at 800 pounds; the City finally paid 843 pounds. — LPC LP-0548, 1970
128. On 9 July 1776 — the day the Declaration of Independence reached New York from Philadelphia and was read to the troops — a crowd of soldiers and civilians pulled the statue down and hacked it apart. — LPC LP-0548, 1970; NYC Parks, n.d.
129. The reading was in "the Fields" (City Hall Park); the crowd then proceeded down Broadway. — NYC Parks, "The Oldest Parks," n.d.
130. NPS states the toppled statue "was melted into two tons of metal and turned into 42,088 musket balls for George Washington's Continental army." — NPS, "Bowling Green," n.d.
131. The WPA Guide states plainly that "the lead statue of George III in Bowling Green had been melted into bullets for the Colonists' cause." — WPA 1939
132. LPC on the fence-post ornaments: "The small ornaments, which originally capped the posts and were variously described as royal crowns or iron balls, were broken off by patriots and used for ammunition." — LPC LP-0548, 1970
133. The WPA Guide hedges differently: "During the Revolution, the royal crowns ornamenting the fence pickets disappeared." — WPA 1939
134. The NYC Parks bronze plaque at the site (erected 1964 by the New York Community Trust) hedges explicitly: patriots "ARE SAID TO HAVE / REMOVED THE CROWNS WHICH CAPPED THE / FENCE POSTS BUT THE FENCE ITSELF REMAINS." — NYC Parks, "Bowling Green Fence Plaque," n.d. https://www.nycgovparks.org/parks/bowling-green/monuments/139
135. The fence itself was also partially pulled down during the Revolution and was repaired in 1786. — LPC LP-0548, 1970
136. In 1791 the fence and its stone bedding were raised two feet above the original level. — LPC LP-0548, 1970
137. In 1819 the Common Council let neighbouring residents plant trees and shrubs; in 1825 the Green was "laid down in grass." — LPC LP-0548, 1970
138. E. Porter Belden in 1850: "one entire post, with its head upon its shoulders, still stands near the southern entrance to the Green, a memento of the former glory of its brethren." — quoted in LPC LP-0548, 1970 (Belden, 1850 — public domain)
139. Belden also described the Green as "an ellipse, whose diameters are respectively two hundred and twenty and one hundred and forty feet." — quoted in LPC LP-0548, 1970
140. Belden's account is corroborated by David Johnson's 1860 painting at the New-York Historical Society, which also holds a small section of the fence. — LPC LP-0548, 1970
141. The fence was dismantled in 1914 for subway construction, removed to Central Park, forgotten, and mostly rediscovered and restored to Bowling Green in 1919. — LPC LP-0548, 1970
142. The Bowling Green Fence was designated a New York City Landmark on 14 July 1970 (LP-0548) as "a unique survivor of the period immediately preceding the American Revolution." — LPC LP-0548, 1970

144. The WPA Guide uses the same hedge: "Here, according to the legend, astute Peter Minuit made the bargain..." — WPA 1939

## CONTESTED OR SHAKY CLAIMS — FLAGGED

1. **"Six columns or eight?" (Federal Hall porticoes).** HABS NY-470's 1935 description says *hexastyle* (six). The 1966/1972 NRHP form says "the eight Doric columns at each end," and an 1840 newspaper account quoted inside the HABS file says "porticos of eight columns each." What's safe to voice: "a colonnaded Doric portico at each end." If you want a number, count them on site before recording.
2. **"Modelled on the Parthenon."** What the sources actually support: the 1833 competition entry was described as *in proportion with* the Parthenon, and period/NPS descriptions say the colonnade and low pediment *recall* the Parthenon, "something after the model of." It is not a copy — the Parthenon is octastyle with a peristyle on all sides; this is a rectangular masonry building with end porticoes and a domed Corinthian rotunda inside. Say "recalls" or "in the spirit of," not "a replica of."
3. **"The 1842 Custom House, by Town & Davis."** Half true, and the standard line. Town & Davis won the competition and set the exterior; William Ross redesigned the interior; John Frazee superintended construction, drew most of the details, and is "generally known as the architect" (HABS). The NRHP form credits Frazee alone. Safest narration: "designed by Town and Davis, reworked by William Ross and built under John Frazee."
4. **The NRHP form's "statue of George Washington... in 1833."** That is an error (or an OCR artifact) in the 1972 nomination text. J.Q.A. Ward's Washington was erected in **1883** — WPA 1939 and NPS both put it in the 1880s. Do not voice 1833.
5. **"Washington stood on this exact spot."** The WPA Guide phrases it as "the place above the steps where it is claimed he stood." The 1789 building was demolished in 1812; the present building is not it. What is documented is that the balcony *stone* survives and is displayed inside. Voice it as "the site," not "the spot," unless you're describing the stone.
6. **"So help me God."** DO NOT voice this as something Washington said. Contemporary press accounts indicate he did not add the phrase; the Senate Historical Office attributed it to him in 2000 and **no longer does**. — National Archives, "Abrupt Transition," *Prologue*, Winter 2000 https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/2000/winter/abrupt-transition ; Library of Congress Law Library blog, "Oath of Office," 2015 https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2015/01/oath-of-office/
7. **"Long live George Washington, President of the United States."** Widely repeated as Livingston's cry to the crowd. I could NOT verify it against a primary source in this pass (no gov/PD citation obtained). Drop it or attribute it as "the story goes."
8. **Founding date of the original building: 1699 vs 1699–1703.** NPS's current page says construction ran 1699–1703; the 1972 NRHP form and the WPA Guide both say 1699. Say "around 1700" and you cannot be wrong.
9. **Trinity's spire: 281 feet or 280?** Trinity's own history says 281; the 1939 WPA Guide says 280 "above the steps" — which may be the same measurement taken from a different datum. "About 280 feet" is safe.
10. **"Tallest building in America."** Trinity's own claim is tallest in the US 1846–1869 (surpassed by St. Michael's Church, Chicago) and tallest in New York until 1890 (New York World Building, 348 ft). Both are *church-spire-era* claims, and the earlier Trinity of 1790 had a 200-ft steeple, so "tallest thing in the city" language is comparative, not absolute. Say "for a generation it was the tallest structure in the city" and cite the 1890 World Building if you want the end date. Note one search result gave the World Building as 309 ft rather than 348 — Trinity's page says 348.
11. **"Hamilton worshipped at Trinity."** Trinity itself says its records do NOT establish that he attended regularly; Eliza appears on an 1801 communion list. He is *buried* there — that is the documented fact.
12. **"Trinity Church Cemetery" vs "Trinity Churchyard."** Two different places. Hamilton, Fulton and Lawrence are in the **churchyard** at Broadway and Wall. Trinity Church Cemetery — where Audubon, Astor and Ellison lie — is uptown at 153rd–155th Streets, laid out in 1843. Don't let a source about one become narration about the other.
13. **9/11 pool dimensions.** The institution says "approximately 200 feet" per side and "nearly an acre" each. The often-repeated "176 feet" is not what the Memorial publishes; avoid it. The 30-foot and 20-foot drops are the institution's own numbers.
14. **"About 400 swamp white oaks."** The Memorial's own backgrounder says **413**. Either say 413 or say "more than four hundred."
15. **"1,200 meaningful adjacency requests were all honored."** The institution's phrasings differ slightly: "almost all 1,200 requests" in one place, "more than 1,200 requests were honored" in the backgrounder. Say "more than 1,200 requests were honored" (the backgrounder is the newer, cited document) or use the softer "almost all."
16. **Survivor Tree height figures.** Frequently quoted "eight feet when recovered, thirty now" — the Memorial's own materials do NOT give heights. I could not source them. Omit numbers; describe the demarcation line between old and new growth, which the institution does document.
17. **Bowling Green "first public park, 1733."** NYC Parks says first *official* park, by Common Council resolution of 12 March 1733. The WPA Guide dates the lease to **1732** and calls it "probably the city's oldest public park." The discrepancy is likely Old Style/New Style dating (the legal year began 25 March), i.e. the same event. Voice "1733" with NYC Parks behind you, and don't claim "the first public park in America."
18. **The crowns on the fence posts.** This is the single shakiest item on the walk and every official source hedges it. LPC: the caps "were variously described as royal crowns or iron balls" and were "broken off by patriots." The 1964 city plaque: patriots "are said to have" removed them. WPA 1939: they simply "disappeared." Documented: the caps are gone and the fence is the 1771 original. Tradition: that they were crowns, and that patriots took them off in a revolutionary gesture. Narrate the hedge — it's a better line than the certainty.
19. **"42,088 musket balls."** NPS states this figure flatly ("two tons of metal... 42,088 musket balls"). It ultimately derives from an 18th-century tally associated with the Litchfield, Connecticut melting, not from a modern audit; note also that NPS says two tons while other accounts say ~4,000 lb of lead (the same order of magnitude). It is safe to voice **with attribution** — "the Park Service puts it at 42,088 musket balls" — and unsafe to voice as an independently verified count.
20. **"All of the statue became bullets."** Not true, and the survival of fragments proves it: the New-York Historical Society holds pieces including the horse's tail, and parts were reportedly carried off or sent to England. I could not load the New-York Historical blog page (HTTP 403) to confirm details, so treat the head-to-Lord-Townshend story as unverified here. Safe narration: "most of the lead went for ammunition; pieces of it survive in a museum a few miles uptown."
21. **The Minuit "sale of Manhattan" at Bowling Green.** NPS labels it "legendary" and "according to tradition"; the WPA Guide says "according to the legend." If you use it at all, use their hedge.
22. **The 1776 Great Fire's cause.** Disputed in 1776 and never resolved — arson by patriots, or accident. Trinity's own history says no theory was proven. Don't assign blame.
23. **WPA Guide copyright.** The 1939 *New York City Guide* was produced by the Federal Writers' Project but published commercially and copyrighted by the Guilds' Committee for Federal Writers' Publications, Inc. It is widely treated as public domain (no renewal found in common use), but it is NOT public domain by virtue of being a federal work the way NPS and Library of Congress material is. For anything you want to quote verbatim on air, prefer the NPS / LOC / NARA / LPC sources, all of which are government-produced.

## PUBLIC-DOMAIN FIRST-HAND QUOTATIONS WORTH SPEAKING

**Washington's First Inaugural Address, Federal Hall, 30 April 1789** (public domain; National Archives transcription https://www.archives.gov/exhibits/american_originals/inaugtxt.html):
> "And since the preservation of the sacred fire of liberty, and the destiny of the Republican model of Government, are justly considered as deeply, perhaps as finally staked, on the experiment entrusted to the hands of the American people."

**Washington, same address, on the coming amendments** (public domain; quoted by the Library of Congress, *Creating the United States* exhibit):
> "a reverence for the characteristic rights of freemen, and a regard for the public harmony"

**Chief Justice James DeLancey, at the Zenger trial, 1735** (public domain; quoted by NPS):
> "The laws in my opinion are very clear; they cannot be admitted to justify a libel."

**Declaration of Rights and Grievances, Stamp Act Congress, New York, 19 October 1765** (public domain; quoted by the Library of Congress):
> "it is inseparably essential to the freedom of the people, and the undoubted right of Englishmen, that no taxes should be imposed on them but with their own consent, given personally or by their representatives."

**Alexander Hamilton to Elizabeth Hamilton, before the duel, 1804** (public domain; quoted by Trinity Church):
> "The scruples of a Christian have determined me to expose my own life to any extent rather than subject myself to the guilt of taking the life of another."

**Hamilton to his rector, Benjamin Moore, dying, 12 July 1804** (public domain; quoted by Trinity Church):
> "I have no ill-will against Col. Burr. I met him with a fixed resolution to do him no harm — I forgive all that happened."

**Contemporary newspaper on Trinity's steeple burning, September 1776** (public domain; quoted by Trinity Church):
> "The Steeple, which was 140 feet high, the upper Part of Wood... resembled a vast Pyramid of Fire."

**The Bowling Green lease, Common Council, 1733** (public domain; quoted by NYC Parks):
> "for the Beauty and Ornament of the Said Street as well as for the Recreation & Delight of the Inhabitants of this City"

**E. Porter Belden on the last crowned fence post, 1850** (public domain; quoted in LPC LP-0548):
> "one entire post, with its head upon its shoulders, still stands near the southern entrance to the Green, a memento of the former glory of its brethren."

**The 1964 plaque at Bowling Green — worth reading aloud as an artifact of careful hedging** (NYC Parks, "Bowling Green Fence Plaque"):
> "ERECTED BY COMMON COUNCIL IN 1771... THE PARK WAS LEASED IN 1733 FOR USE AS A BOWLING GREEN AT A RENTAL OF ONE PEPPERCORN A YEAR. PATRIOTS, WHO IN 1776 DESTROYED AN EQUESTRIAN STATUE OF GEORGE III WHICH STOOD HERE, ARE SAID TO HAVE REMOVED THE CROWNS WHICH CAPPED THE FENCE POSTS BUT THE FENCE ITSELF REMAINS."

**Barber & Howe, *Historical Collections of the State of New York*, 1841, on the new Custom House** (public domain; quoted in HABS NY-470). The HABS scan of this passage is badly OCR-degraded, so treat the wording as approximate and verify against the 1841 original before voicing it verbatim. Substance: the building "surpasses any building of its size in the world, both in the beauty of its design and the durability of its construction"; 200 ft long, 90 ft wide, about 60 ft high; rotunda 60 ft clear; sixteen polished marble Corinthian columns 30 ft high; largest marble blocks 33 tons; "there is not a particle of woodwork in any part of the building."
